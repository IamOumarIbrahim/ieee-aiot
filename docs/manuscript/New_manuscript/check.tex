\begin{abstract}
Frequent false alarms in edge-deployed driver monitoring systems degrade driver trust, waste on-device computing cycles, and elevate thermal load. This paper evaluates whether curating negative training frames reduces false alarms and whether replacing randomly sampled negative frames with hard-mined false positives provides further suppression. Four lightweight You Only Look Once (YOLO) object detection models were trained and benchmarked on a 15,723-frame dataset extracted from the Driver Monitoring Dataset across four localized visual cues using a dedicated graphics processing unit. Models were evaluated across negative-frame ratios ranging from 0\% to 80\%, followed by hard-negative mining trials and an execution latency benchmark measuring per-frame runtime against standard 30\,ms real-time in-cabin deadlines, providing an edge-proxy compute baseline prior to low-power embedded deployment. Results demonstrate  that increasing negative exposure consistently reduces false positives across all evaluated detectors, achieving a 6.4× to 25.7× reduction at the 80\% configuration while maintaining stable detection performance. At the 40\% reference ratio, hard negative mining provides an additional 33.3\% to 67.8\% reduction in false positives compared with random negative sampling, with the magnitude of improvement varying across models. Runtime profiling demonstrates sub 30 ms inference latency under batch size one execution without modifying model architecture or inference complexity. These results demonstrate that negative frame curation and hard negative mining provide an effective and computationally inexpensive approach for improving the operational reliability of lightweight edge Driver Monitoring Systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Beginning 7~July~2024, European Union (EU) regulations required Advanced Driver Distraction Warning (ADDW) systems in newly type-approved vehicles, and this mandate extends to all newly registered vehicles from 7~July~2026 \cite{ec2026safercars}. 
Early vision-based Driver Monitoring Systems (DMS) estimated driver states using spatio-temporal action recognition \cite{ortega2020dmd, canas2021detection}. 
However, these three-dimensional convolutional architectures require excessive memory and compute time, making them unsuitable for low-power automotive microcontrollers \cite{canas2021detection, florez2024real}. 
Modern edge DMS pipelines instead formulate monitoring as frame-by-frame object detection \cite{yolodrive2025, pyolov82024}. 
These pipelines deploy lightweight models such as the You Only Look Once (YOLO) detector family to identify localized behavioral cues, including phone use, drinking, and eye closure \cite{ortega2020dmd, yolov12dms2025}. 
In-cabin edge hardware must run these models under strict limits: inference latency must remain under 30\,ms \cite{liu2019edge}, and power draw must stay below 10--15\,W to avoid overheating \cite{florez2024real, benoitcattin2020thermal}. 

Lightweight detectors meet these power and latency targets, but existing DMS datasets consist almost entirely of positive distraction events \cite{yolodrive2025, pyolov82024}. 
Because single-frame detectors lack temporal context, models trained without negative examples generate frequent false positives on benign objects like steering wheel contours, seat belts, and clothing folds \cite{canas2021detection}. 
Naturalistic driving studies indicate that secondary-task engagement occupies only a small share of total driving time. In particular, Stutts \textit{et al.} observed non-driving activities, excluding passenger conversation, during 14.5\% of driving time \cite{stutts2005}. Baseline, cue-free driving therefore represents the substantial majority of on-road operation. Our evaluation stream standardizes background prevalence at $p_{\mathrm{neg}} = 0.809$ (80.9\% negative frames, matching our held-out test split) to benchmark false-alarm suppression under controlled in-cabin conditions. 
Frequent false alarms during normal driving cause alert fatigue, leading drivers to ignore, mute, or bypass safety systems entirely \cite{lambert2026chinese, parasuraman1997humans}. 
These false alarms also trigger downstream tasks, including facial landmark tracking, Controller Area Network (CAN) bus logging, and video recording \cite{florez2024real, liu2019edge}. 
These secondary tasks create processing spikes that cause thermal throttling on embedded chips \cite{benoitcattin2020thermal}. 
Adding negative (background-only) frames during training reduces false alarms, but the impact of negative-sampling ratios and hard-negative mining has not been thoroughly evaluated in edge DMS applications \cite{ultralytics2023guidelines, jin2018video}. 

To address these challenges, this paper investigates two core research questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Negative Sampling Ratio):} What ratio of negative-to-positive training frames minimizes false alarms without degrading cue detection sensitivity across lightweight models?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Hard-Negative Mining):} Does replacing random background frames with mined false positives improve suppression at matched training volume?
\end{itemize}
This study delivers three main contributions: (1) a systematic ratio sweep (0\% to 80\%) across four compact YOLO architectures (YOLO11n, YOLO26n, YOLO12n, and YOLOv10n); (2) a closed-loop hard-negative mining evaluation demonstrating up to 67.8\% additional false-alarm reduction without accuracy loss; and (3) an operational alert-rate formulation ($\mathcal{A}_h$) linking frame-level false positives to hourly alert frequencies and edge thermal overheads.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Vision-Based Driver Monitoring and Cue Detection}
Vision-based DMS historically formulated driver state estimation as action recognition \cite{ortega2020dmd, canas2021detection}. Early methods used convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to classify video clips into coarse categories, such as safe driving, talking, or drinking \cite{ortega2020dmd, canas2021detection}. Multimodal benchmarks, including the Driver Monitoring Dataset (DMD) \cite{ortega2020dmd} and Drive\&Act \cite{martin2019drive}, established standardized evaluation protocols across red-green-blue (RGB), depth, and infrared camera streams. However, 3D CNNs and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks impose latency and memory requirements that exceed automotive microcontroller limits \cite{canas2021detection, florez2024real}.

To satisfy real-time constraints, modern edge DMS pipelines reframe monitoring as frame-by-frame object detection using lightweight YOLO models \cite{yolodrive2025, pyolov82024}. Recent works propose customized architectures: YOLO-Drive incorporates state-space blocks and attention mechanisms \cite{yolodrive2025}, P-YOLOv8 leverages a pretrained YOLOv8 backbone with a lightweight classification head optimized for TinyML deployment \cite{pyolov82024}, and Chaman \textit{et al.} benchmark compact YOLOv12 models \cite{yolov12dms2025}. Yet, these detectors are trained almost entirely on positive distraction intervals \cite{yolodrive2025, pyolov82024}. Without background regularization, single-frame models lack temporal context and frequently misclassify steering wheels, seat belts, and clothing folds as distraction cues \cite{canas2021detection}. Rather than designing bespoke architectures, this work introduces a data-centric evaluation of negative-frame curation and hard-negative mining to suppress false alarms across standard YOLO detectors.

\subsection{Automotive Edge Computing and Cascading Thermal Overhead}
Automotive edge platforms must maintain sub-30\,ms inference within a 10--15\,W power budget under passive cooling \cite{florez2024real, liu2019edge}. Embedded DMS studies typically evaluate frame rates and nominal power under static workloads \cite{florez2024real}. In deployed automotive pipelines, however, cue detections trigger secondary tasks, including facial landmark alignment, gaze tracking, CAN bus logging, and video ring-buffer encoding \cite{florez2024real, liu2019edge}.

When an unregularized detector produces frequent false alarms, these secondary tasks execute repeatedly. Benoit-Cattin \textit{et al.} demonstrated that sustained processing bursts cause thermal throttling in embedded vision processors, triggering dynamic frequency scaling that reduces inference throughput by over 40\% \cite{benoitcattin2020thermal}. Existing DMS benchmarks evaluate detectors in isolation, overlooking how frame-level false positives compound into operational hourly nuisance alerts ($\mathcal{A}_h$) that induce thermal stress.

\subsection{Alert Fatigue, Automation Disuse, and Regulatory Mandates}
European Union (EU) vehicle-safety regulations require reliable distraction detection and prompt warning escalation \cite{ec2026safercars}. However, persistent false alarms compromise safety system efficacy. In human-automation interaction theory, Parasuraman and Riley established that false alarms drive automation \textit{disuse}, defined as the intentional muting, ignoring, or disabling of safety alerts by operators \cite{parasuraman1997humans}. Repeated false alerts during routine driving induce alert fatigue and erode driver trust, motivating motorists to circumvent monitoring hardware with aftermarket defeat devices \cite{lambert2026chinese, parasuraman1997humans}. Suppressing false alarms is therefore essential for driver acceptance and regulatory compliance.

\subsection{Negative-Frame Curation and Hard-Negative Mining}
In object detection, false-positive suppression typically relies on loss reweighting, such as Focal Loss \cite{lin2017focal}, or candidate resampling, such as Online Hard Example Mining (OHEM) \cite{shrivastava2016ohem}. However, online anchor-level mining like OHEM is computationally impractical for lightweight single-stage architectures, and loss reweighting cannot compensate when training sets lack background negative frames entirely \cite{lin2017focal}. Instead, offline image-level bootstrapping provides a practical alternative: false-positive frames are harvested from unannotated streams to augment subsequent training passes.

Practitioner heuristics often recommend adding 0\% to 10\% background frames during training \cite{ultralytics2023guidelines}. Yet, this guideline was designed for general object detection benchmarks and remains unvalidated in automotive environments, where naturalistic on-road evidence indicates that potentially distracting secondary-task engagement occupies only a small share of total driving time \cite{stutts2005}, yet standard object-detection benchmarks under-sample these baseline, cue-free periods. Furthermore, uniform random negative sampling yields diminishing returns because simple background scenes generate minimal gradient feedback \cite{shrivastava2016ohem, jin2018video}. While video-based mining techniques harvest temporal variations as hard negatives in generic domains \cite{jin2018video}, prior works have not systematically evaluated negative ratios from 0\% to 80\% across lightweight YOLO detectors or measured the impact of hard-negative mining on operational nuisance alert rates ($\mathcal{A}_h$).

\subsection{Dataset Curation and Annotation Protocol}
\label{subsec:data}
We extract 15,723 face-crop frames at 1 frame per second (FPS) from 81 red-green-blue (RGB) recordings across 14 drivers in the Driver Monitoring Dataset (DMD) \cite{ortega2020dmd}. The curated set contains 3,001 positive cue frames (19.1\%) and 12,722 negative frames (80.9\%), establishing a background-heavy test stream that approximates the operational rarity of distraction events. Negative frames contain no bounding boxes. Positive frames contain exactly one localized bounding box identifying one of four driver cues: yawning, hand-over-mouth, drinking, or phone use (Fig.~\ref{fig:combined_framework}(a)).

Annotations were generated by a single annotator under three rules:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mutual Exclusion:} If a driver covers a yawn with a hand, the behavior is labeled exclusively as hand-over-mouth.
    \item \textbf{Visible Anatomy Only:} Bounding boxes enclose only directly visible cue interactions without extrapolating occluded joints.
    \item \textbf{Scale Invariance:} No minimum bounding-box pixel dimension is imposed at the $640 \times 640$ resolution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics and False-Alarm Quantification}
\label{subsec:metrics}
Models are evaluated across detection precision, recall, F1 score, mean Average Precision (mAP), false alarm rate (FAR), and false positives per 1,000 frames (FP/1k). Detections undergo non-maximum suppression (NMS) using an IoU threshold of 0.70 and an operational confidence threshold of $\tau = 0.25$. We note that while false alarms on a positive-only model can be partially reduced by simply raising $\tau$ post-hoc, doing so severely degrades foreground recall for subtle behavioral cues (such as partial eye closure or occluded phone use); background negative regularization is therefore required to suppress false alarms at fixed operational sensitivity.

A prediction is scored as a True Positive (TP) if it predicts the correct cue class with an Intersection over Union (IoU) $\ge 0.50$ against a ground-truth box; otherwise, it is a False Positive (FP). Ground-truth boxes with no matching detection are False Negatives (FN). Precision, recall, and optimal F1 score are defined as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Precision} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}}, \quad \text{Recall} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}},
\label{eq:precision_recall}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
F1 = 2 \cdot \frac{\text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}.
\label{eq:f1}
\end{equation}

Localization quality is measured using $\text{mAP}_{50}$ across the $C = 4$ driver-cue classes:
\begin{equation}
\text{mAP}_{50} = \frac{1}{C} \sum_{c=1}^{C} \text{AP}_c^{50},
\label{eq:map}
\end{equation}
where $\text{AP}_c^{50}$ is the Average Precision for class $c$ at an IoU threshold of 0.50. We also report $\text{mAP}_{50:95}$, averaged across ten IoU thresholds from $0.50$ to $0.95$ in $0.05$ increments.

To quantify false alerts on background scenes, let $FP_{\mathrm{neg}}$ denote detections with confidence $\ge \tau$ ($\tau = 0.25$) produced on the $N_{\mathrm{neg}} = 1{,}272$ unannotated test frames ($\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{eval}}^{\mathrm{neg}}$). Per-frame FAR and scaled operational FP/1k are defined as:
\begin{equation}
\text{FAR} = \frac{FP_{\mathrm{neg}}}{N_{\mathrm{neg}}}, \qquad \text{FP/1k} = 1{,}000 \times \text{FAR}.
\label{eq:far}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Hard-Negative Mining Protocol}
\label{subsec:mining}
To address RQ2, we implement the closed-loop hard-negative mining protocol shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:combined_framework}(b). We first train a baseline model $f_{\theta_0}$ using the 0\% negative baseline (2,401 positive frames). Lacking background regularization, $f_{\theta_0}$ readily triggers on benign cabin features, serving as an effective filter for discovering informative non-target patterns.

We run $f_{\theta_0}$ in batched inference over unselected training background frames ($\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{neg}}$, strictly isolated from validation and test sets). For frame $X \in \mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{neg}}$, let $\mathrm{conf}(X)$ denote the maximum detection confidence. Frames triggering false detections with $\mathrm{conf}(X) \ge \tau$ ($\tau = 0.25$) are harvested as hard negatives:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{mined}} = \left\{ X \in \mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{neg}} \;\middle|\; \mathrm{conf}(X) \ge \tau \right\}.
\label{eq:mining_set}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Real-Time Deployment and Runtime Overhead Evaluation}
\label{subsec:deployment}
To quantify operational impact, we project empirical FAR into expected hourly nuisance alerts, $\mathcal{A}_h$. For camera streaming rate $r$ FPS, one hour of driving (3,600\,s), and an evaluation background driving prevalence of $p_{\mathrm{neg}} \approx 0.809$, assuming independent per-frame inference:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A}_h = 3600 \times r \times p_{\mathrm{neg}} \times \text{FAR}.
\label{eq:ah}
\end{equation}
While real-world false detections from static cabin features (such as seatbelt brackets or steering wheel seams) often occur in temporally correlated bursts rather than independent Bernoulli trials, \eqref{eq:ah} provides an unmitigated upper-bound rate for comparing raw detector false-alarm resistance. We evaluate $\mathcal{A}_h$ across three streaming rates: $r \in \{5, 15, 30\}$\,FPS, corresponding to low-power sampling, typical embedded vision capture, and full real-time streaming.

\textbf{Convergence at High Ratios:} Detectors trained without negative frames (0\%) produce high false-alarm rates of 45.20 to 80.59\,FP/1k (Fig.~\ref{fig:ratio_sweep}). Increasing negative frames to 80\% reduces false alarms across all models to a narrow range of 3.14 to 7.08\,FP/1k, representing a $6.4\times$ to $25.7\times$ reduction. Across all 20 evaluated configurations, the negative ratio strongly correlates inversely with FP/1k (Spearman $\rho = -0.937, p < 10^{-4}$). We note that these 20 points are not fully independent observations, since each of the four architectures contributes five ratios (a repeated-measures structure); the reported $p$-values should be read as descriptive rather than as formal hypothesis tests, as no correction for this clustering is applied.

\textbf{Cue Sensitivity and Localization Stability:} Precision improves significantly with negative ratio (Spearman $\rho = +0.546, p < 0.05$) due directly to false-alarm suppression. In contrast, recall remains uncorrelated with negative ratio (Spearman $\rho = +0.012, p > 0.50$), confirming that negative frames do not cause under-detection. Localization accuracy ($\text{mAP}_{50:95}$) remains within a narrow operational band across all configurations (0.7086--0.7766), showing no systematic degradation with increasing negative exposure.

\textbf{Intermediate Trajectory Differences:} Although all models converge at 80\% negatives, their trajectories differ between 20\% and 60\%. YOLO26n drops sharply to 16.90\,FP/1k at 20\% negatives, plateauing early. In contrast, YOLO11n and YOLO12n show a more gradual decline requiring ratios between 60\% and 80\%, whereas YOLOv10n achieves early suppression by 40\% (14.94\,FP/1k) before reaching its 4.72\,FP/1k floor at 60\%.

\textbf{Alert Fatigue and Automation Disuse:} Models trained without negative frames (0\%) trigger impractically high alert frequencies (Table~\ref{tab:nuisance_alerts}). At 30\,FPS, unregularized detectors trigger 3{,}949 to 7{,}041 alerts/hr (1--2 false alerts per second). Even at 5\,FPS, they generate 658 to 1{,}174 alerts/hr. In human-automation interaction theory, this alert frequency exceeds driver tolerance, directly inducing alert fatigue and automation disuse \cite{lambert2026chinese, parasuraman1997humans}.

\textbf{Cascading Edge Compute and Thermal Load:} Continuous false alarms persistently trigger downstream facial tracking, logging, and video encoding routines. The resulting processing spikes can induce thermal throttling and reduce edge throughput by over 40\% \cite{florez2024real, benoitcattin2020thermal}.

\textbf{Mitigation via Negative Curation:} Curating negative training frames lowers projected alert rates by up to 96.1\%. At an 80\% ratio, alerts drop to 274--619\,alerts/hr at 30\,FPS and 46--103\,alerts/hr at 5\,FPS. Furthermore, hard-negative mining provides comparable suppression with one-third the dataset volume and one-sixth the negative frames: at 40\%, hard-mined models produce 550--619\,alerts/hr at 30\,FPS, an 86\% to 92\% reduction from the 0\% baseline depending on architecture.

These projections highlight an engineering distinction: \textit{inference latency depends on architecture, whereas operational reliability depends on data curation}. While all four models execute within real-time limits (7.7--21.7\,ms; Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_throughput}), negative curation determines whether a detector avoids alert fatigue and thermal throttling.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Architectural Mechanisms and Practical Curation Guidelines}
\label{subsec:guidelines}
A detector's feature-aggregation mechanism determines how it responds to negative-frame curation.

\textbf{Mechanistic Divergence:} Random negative sampling primarily selects low-entropy background patches that generate minimal training gradients. Purely convolutional networks, such as YOLO26n, rely on localized $3 \times 3$ re-parameterized convolutions (RepConv). These layers quickly learn repetitive cabin textures, causing YOLO26n to plateau at a 20\% negative ratio and gain modest benefit from hard-negative mining ($-33.3\%$). In contrast, models using self-attention (YOLO11n), area attention (YOLO12n), or dual-label assignment (YOLOv10n) capture wider spatial context. Without negative frames, attention layers often mistake cabin lighting or seat contours for driver cues. Mined hard negatives provide high-entropy distractors that force attention heads to distinguish cabin structures from driver actions. For YOLOv10n, mined negatives also penalize over-assignment in the auxiliary head, sharpening the decision boundary of the primary inference branch.

\textbf{Per-Seed Stability:} In our two-seed evaluations, attention-based and dual-assignment models showed consistent suppression across runs, whereas YOLO26n exhibited marked variability (ranging from 0.0\% under Seed~42 to 47.0\% under Seed~43). While this suggests potential seed sensitivity in localized convolutional backbones, evaluations across a larger battery of seeds are required before drawing definitive architectural conclusions.

\textbf{Practical Curation Guidelines:} These empirical results establish two engineering guidelines for edge driver monitoring systems (DMS):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Backbone-Targeted Curation:} Uniform random sampling provides sufficient false-alarm suppression for convolutional backbones. However, attention-centric and dual-assignment models require hard-negative mining, yielding an additional 52.6\% to 67.8\% false-alarm reduction at matched training compute.
    \item \textit{Prevalence Trade-Offs (40\% vs. 80\%):} An 80\% negative ratio achieves the lowest false-alarm floor for most architectures (3.14--4.72\,FP/1k), though YOLO12n reaches its minimum under 40\% hard mining (6.29\,FP/1k). Where storage or compute is constrained, a 40\% hard-mined ratio approaches 80\% random suppression (6.29--7.08\,FP/1k) while requiring only one-third the total dataset volume and one-sixth the negative frames.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}
This evaluation has six primary limitations:

\textbf{Single-Annotator Labeling:} A single annotator labeled the visual cues without an independent review, which may introduce subjective bias in ambiguous edge cases.

\textbf{Simplified Cue Annotation and Mutual Exclusion:} Annotations enforced a single bounding box per positive frame and a strict mutual-exclusion rule between overlapping behaviors (e.g., covering a yawn is labeled exclusively as hand-over-mouth). In naturalistic driving, concurrent and overlapping cues frequently co-occur; this protocol simplifies the detection objective and may understate real-world false negatives when simultaneous behaviors arise.

\textbf{Single-Modality RGB Sensing:} The evaluation relies entirely on visible-light RGB video feeds without near-infrared (NIR) or active depth sensing commonly deployed for in-cabin night vision.

\textbf{Isolated Frame-Level Inference:} Detections were evaluated per frame without temporal persistence filtering (e.g., Kalman tracking or sliding time-windows), yielding an unmitigated upper-bound alert baseline.

\textbf{Driver Partition Overlap and Single-Pass Mining:} Splits were stratified across cue instances rather than subject-disjoint driver identities. Because frames were extracted at 1 FPS from continuous recordings, near-duplicate background frames from the same driver and session may appear in both a training split and a held-out split; this could inflate the reported false-alarm suppression relative to a fully subject-disjoint evaluation and should be treated as an upper bound on generalization until a driver-held-out replication is run. Hard-negative mining was also restricted to a single closed-loop iteration rather than multi-round continuous bootstrapping.

\textbf{Desktop-GPU Deployment Proxy:} Latency and memory profiling (Section~\ref{subsec:deployment}) was performed on a desktop-class discrete GPU rather than an automotive-grade embedded SoC, and no power draw was measured. Absolute latency, memory, and thermal margins on production in-cabin hardware may differ from the desktop-proxy figures reported here.

\section{Conclusion}
This study evaluated negative-frame curation and hard-negative mining to suppress false alarms in edge-deployed Driver Monitoring Systems (DMS). Across four You Only Look Once (YOLO) architectures with fewer than 3~million parameters, increasing negative training frames from 0\% to 80\% reduced false positives per 1{,}000 frames (FP/1k) by $6.4\times$ to $25.7\times$. Core detection sensitivity ($\text{mAP}_{50:95}$) and recall remained stable while precision increased. At matched dataset volume, replacing random negative frames with hard-mined false positives achieved an additional 33.3\% to 67.8\% false-alarm reduction. Attention-based and dual-assignment models benefited most from hard negatives, whereas purely convolutional models achieved effective suppression using random background frames alone. In streaming projections, these curation protocols lowered nuisance alerts below 620\,alerts/hr at 30\,frames per second (FPS), mitigating driver alert fatigue and edge computational load.

These findings show that dataset curation is as critical as architectural design for edge deployment. Furthermore, curation strategies must match the detector's internal feature-aggregation mechanism: attention heads require high-entropy mined distractors to eliminate false correlations, whereas convolutional kernels regularize effectively on uniform background scenes. 

This study has six primary shortcomings: visual cues were labeled by a single annotator; annotations enforced single-cue mutual exclusion rather than simultaneous multi-cue interactions; evaluations relied exclusively on single-frame visible-light RGB feeds without temporal smoothing; mining was limited to a single pass; data splits were stratified across cue instances rather than disjoint drivers; and latency/memory profiling relied on a desktop-GPU proxy rather than validated automotive SoC hardware, with no power measurement. Future work should validate these curation protocols on multi-modal near-infrared (NIR) and depth sensors, incorporate multi-frame temporal consensus algorithms, evaluate iterative multi-round mining, test cross-subject generalization on disjoint driver splits, and repeat the latency and power-draw benchmarks on representative automotive SoC hardware.
