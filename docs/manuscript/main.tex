\title{Negative Frames Are Not Architecture-Agnostic: A Cross-Detector
       Study of Negative-Frame Configuration for Edge AIoT Driver Monitoring}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Authors}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Institution redacted for double-blind review}}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Edge AIoT object detectors, such as in-cabin driver-monitoring systems,
continuously process video streams in which background-only (negative) frames
dominate the natural operational distribution (approximately 81\% negative
prevalence). While incorporating negative frames during training mitigates false
positives, prior work tunes the negative-to-positive proportion in isolation for
individual detector architectures, leaving open whether an optimally tuned
configuration transfers across architecturally distinct models. This paper presents
the first controlled, cross-architecture empirical investigation of negative-frame
ratio sensitivity and curation quality across three lightweight edge detectors:
YOLO11n (anchor-free CNN), YOLO26n (next-generation CNN), and D-FINE-N
(transformer-based real-time DETR). Evaluating five nested ratio configurations
(0\% to 80\% negative frames) and a hard-negative mining strategy at matched
dataset size on a 15,723-frame driver-monitoring corpus, we determine whether
the performance-maximizing negative-frame ratio is architecture-invariant or
architecture-specific. We further translate false-positive rates per 1,000 frames
into estimated nuisance-alert rates per hour, demonstrating that training-data
curation directly governs edge deployment usability independently of model
inference latency.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Object detectors deployed in AIoT systems---such as in-cabin driver monitoring,
smart surveillance, and industrial defect inspection---operate under a condition
that standard computer-vision benchmarks rarely reflect: the vast majority of
frames encountered at inference time contain \emph{no} target object.
A driver-monitoring camera streams continuously for hours per trip, yet the events
it must flag (distraction, drowsiness, unsafe behavior) occupy only a small fraction
of that stream.
This asymmetry means a detector's behavior on background-only frames---rather than
its accuracy on object-dense benchmark images---ultimately determines whether the
system is usable in the field.
A model prone to false positives on ordinary footage produces nuisance alerts that
erode user trust, waste edge compute cycles, and, in bandwidth- or
battery-constrained AIoT deployments, trigger unnecessary uplink transmissions or
actuator responses.

The standard mitigation is to include negative (background-only) frames during
training, and prior work confirms this lever is powerful.
Reported optimal negative-frame proportions range from single
digits~\cite{b8} to over 60\% of the training set in safety-critical driving
contexts~\cite{b6}, with performance following a non-monotonic, inverted-U
relationship: too few negatives leave false positives unaddressed, while too many
suppress recall by pushing the detector toward over-conservative predictions~\cite{b5}.
However, every empirical study we are aware of tunes this parameter for a
\emph{single} detector architecture in isolation.
It remains unknown whether a negative-frame configuration---including ratio or the
choice between random and hard-mined negatives---tuned for one architecture
transfers to another, or whether the optimum is itself architecture-dependent.

This is not a minor gap.
AIoT practitioners routinely choose among CNN-based, anchor-free, and
transformer-based lightweight detectors under a shared, fixed data-curation budget.
If the answer to ``how many negative frames do I need'' changes with the architecture
choice, then architecture selection and data-curation decisions cannot be made
independently---as much current practice implicitly assumes.

This paper addresses that gap through a controlled, cross-architecture study of
negative-frame configuration on a real-world, safety-critical AIoT dataset.
Using an in-cabin driver-monitoring corpus with a naturally occurring 19:81
positive-to-negative frame ratio, we train three lightweight detectors spanning
distinct design paradigms (YOLO11n, YOLO26n, and D-FINE-N) under systematically
varied negative-frame ratios and sampling strategies, holding all other training
factors fixed. We ask:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1:} Does the negative-frame ratio that maximizes detection
        performance differ across detector architectures, or does a single
        configuration generalize?
  \item \textbf{RQ2:} At each architecture's best ratio, does replacing randomly
        sampled negatives with hard-mined negatives (frames on which a baseline
        model produces false positives) yield further, architecture-dependent gains?
\end{itemize}

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Cross-architecture negative-frame benchmark.} The first empirical
        sweep of negative-frame ratio sensitivity across three lightweight detectors
        spanning CNN and transformer paradigms, on a real, safety-critical AIoT
        dataset (in-cabin driver monitoring).
  \item \textbf{Hard-negative curation protocol and cost-benefit analysis.} A
        mining strategy targeting false-positive-prone background frames, with a
        controlled comparison against random sampling at matched dataset size.
  \item \textbf{Operational deployment-cost reframing.} A translation of FP-per-frame
        differences into nuisance-alert rates per hour, connecting an accuracy metric
        to a deployment consequence AIoT system designers plan around, while explicitly
        separating inference latency (architecture-bound) from FP rate (training-data-bound).
\end{enumerate}
\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Class Imbalance and Negative Sampling in Object Detection}
Negative sampling has long been recognized as a first-order design choice in
object-detector training. At the loss level, Focal Loss~\cite{b1} was introduced
because the vast number of easy negatives in dense-prediction detectors overwhelms
training signals from the rare foreground class; it addresses imbalance through
adaptive loss reweighting rather than through the ratio of negative instances itself.
Related work shows that negatives are not static targets during training: they can
drift toward foreground appearance as training proceeds, confusing the detector's
foreground-background boundary and motivating dynamic re-selection~\cite{b2}.

In anchor-based versus anchor-free comparisons, the rule used to assign positive
and negative training samples has been shown to have a larger effect on final
performance than the underlying regression formulation~\cite{b3}, reinforcing that
sample assignment---rather than architecture alone---is a primary driver of
detection behavior. At the region level, mining strategies that surface visually
confusable background chips measurably raise mAP relative to training on
positive-only regions~\cite{b4}.

\subsection{Negative-Frame Ratio as a Dataset-Curation Lever}
Standard guidance for one-stage YOLO detectors recommends including roughly 0--10\%
background-only images to reduce false positives~\cite{b8}, without cross-architecture
validation. Direct empirical sweeps reveal a non-monotonic relationship: one study
varying the proportion from 0\% to 40\% across two YOLO variants found that recall
and mAP@50 peaked near 20\% before declining, with excess negatives causing
over-conservative predictions~\cite{b5}. In a safety-critical driving context, a
far higher proportion was needed: raising background images from 10\% to over 60\%
cut false positives by more than 80\% while also reducing false negatives by
25\%~\cite{b6}, suggesting the effective operating point is strongly domain-dependent.
In human--object interaction detection, scaling the negative-to-positive pair ratio
from roughly 10:1 to 1000:1 produced a large, consistent performance gain~\cite{b7},
underscoring how strongly the ``correct'' configuration depends on task structure.

\subsection{Research Gap}
Taken together, this literature establishes that negative-sample configuration
reliably shifts a detector's precision-recall operating point, and that the effective
optimum varies dramatically across tasks and domains. However, in every case reviewed,
the evaluation is conducted on a \emph{single} detector architecture at a time. No
existing study holds negative-frame configuration fixed while \emph{varying} detector
architecture, leaving open the key question of whether a ratio or curation strategy
tuned for one model transfers to another. This is precisely the question AIoT
practitioners face when choosing among architecturally distinct lightweight detectors
under a shared data budget---a gap our work fills directly.
\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Dataset}
Experiments use an in-cabin driver-monitoring (DMS) dataset comprising 15{,}723
total frames, of which 3{,}001 contain annotated driver-cue events
(\texttt{phone\_use}, \texttt{drinking}, \texttt{yawning},
\texttt{hand\_over\_mouth}) and 12{,}722 are background-only, reflecting a natural
negative prevalence of approximately 81\%.
All frames are partitioned using a stratified 80/10/10 random split
($\text{seed}=42$) into disjoint training, validation, and test sets.
Positive frames are stratified across the four driver-cue categories.
Validation and test sets each contain 1{,}572 frames (300 positive, 1{,}272
negative; 80.9\% negative prevalence) and are held fixed across all conditions.
This design is deliberate: evaluating on a natural-distribution test set preserves
the false-positive behavior that negative-frame configuration is intended to address;
an artificially rebalanced test set would mask the primary metric ($\mathrm{FP/1k}$).

\subsection{Detector Architectures}
Three lightweight detectors, selected to span distinct design paradigms relevant
to edge deployment, are evaluated under identical conditions.

\textbf{YOLO11n}~\cite{b9} is an anchor-free, one-stage CNN detector representing
the current mainstream YOLO lineage. It predicts bounding boxes by decoupling
classification and regression heads over a C2f backbone at three spatial scales
without anchor priors.

\textbf{YOLO26n}~\cite{b10} is the succeeding YOLO generation, included to test
whether architectural refinements---including an updated backbone and
reparametrized convolution blocks---alter sensitivity to negative-frame
configuration relative to YOLO11n.

\textbf{D-FINE-N}~\cite{b11} is a transformer-based, query-driven real-time
detector that refines bounding boxes via fine-grained distribution estimation at
each decoder layer, representing set-based prediction rather than dense
anchor-free prediction. It is included to test whether the CNN-vs-transformer
paradigm boundary modulates response to negative-frame manipulation.

All three are evaluated at their smallest (``nano'') variant to reflect realistic
AIoT compute budgets. Table~\ref{tab:architectures} summarizes architecture-level
characteristics. Since inference latency is governed by model structure---not
training-data composition---it is reported as fixed context.

\subsection{Negative-Frame Configurations}
Two independent experimental axes isolate the effect of negative-frame training data.

\noindent\textbf{Axis~1 (RQ1) --- Ratio Sweep.}
For each architecture, five training sets are constructed by holding the full
2{,}401-frame positive core fixed and sampling strictly nested negative subsets at
exact arithmetic 20\%-point steps:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\phantom{0}$0\%: 0 neg.\ / 2{,}401 total (\texttt{train\_00\_pos\_only})
  \item 20\%: 600 neg.\ / 3{,}001 total (\texttt{train\_20\_low\_neg})
  \item 40\%: 1{,}600 neg.\ / 4{,}001 total (\texttt{train\_40\_mod\_neg})
  \item 60\%: 3{,}602 neg.\ / 6{,}003 total (\texttt{train\_60\_high\_neg})
  \item 80\%: 9{,}604 neg.\ / 12{,}005 total (\texttt{train\_80\_max\_neg})
\end{itemize}
Negatives are sampled as strictly nested subsets
($\text{Neg}_{0\%} \subset \text{Neg}_{20\%} \subset \cdots \subset
\text{Neg}_{80\%}$, $\text{seed}=42$), yielding $3 \times 5 = 15$ training runs.
All non-negative-frame factors are held constant within each architecture,
isolating negative-frame ratio as the sole independent variable.

\noindent\textbf{Axis~2 (RQ2) --- Hard-Negative Curation.}
For each architecture, the best-performing ratio from Axis~1 anchors a controlled
comparison: random subsampling vs.\ hard-negative mining at \emph{matched dataset
size}. The mining protocol is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Train a baseline on the positive-only split.
  \item Run inference over the full 10{,}178-frame negative candidate pool.
  \item Retain frames yielding a false-positive at confidence $\tau \geq 0.25$
        (standard driver-monitoring threshold).
  \item Backfill with random negatives if the mined count falls short.
\end{enumerate}
This adds $3 \times 2 = 6$ runs, for \textbf{21 total training runs}.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Each trained model is evaluated on the fixed, natural-distribution test set using
two metric categories.
\emph{Detection accuracy:} Precision (P), Recall (R), mAP@50, and mAP@50:95
under standard COCO evaluation.
\emph{Deployment cost:} False positives per 1{,}000 frames ($\mathrm{FP/1k}$),
which captures nuisance-alert frequency under continuous negative dominance.
Unlike mAP---which aggregates across a swept confidence threshold---FP/1k
directly reflects operational alert behavior at the deployed threshold
($\tau=0.25$), making it the primary metric for AIoT streaming evaluation.

Because each configuration is evaluated via a single training run without repeated
seeds, all comparisons report point estimates evaluating practical magnitude and
trend directionality without inferential hypothesis testing.

The key question is whether the performance-maximizing ratio is the same across all
three architectures or is architecture-dependent. Prior single-architecture literature
suggests a non-monotonic curve peaking near 20\% for generic detection~\cite{b5}

YOLO26n, and D-FINE-N, architecture and curation decisions are jointly determined;
if consistent, the ratio is a dataset-level property.

An additional comparison is afforded by including both YOLO11n and YOLO26n: if
these two CNN-based models---sharing design lineage but differing in architectural
detail---respond differently to ratio variation, it indicates that even within the
CNN paradigm, curation configurations cannot simply be inherited across generations.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Practical Implications for AIoT Edge Deployment}
The central question---whether negative-frame configuration can be tuned once and
applied across detector architectures, or must be re-tuned per architecture---has
direct operational implications for AIoT development teams.

\textit{If results confirm architecture-dependent optima,} architecture selection
and negative-frame curation are not independent decisions. A practitioner who tunes
the negative-frame ratio on YOLO11n and then swaps to YOLO26n or D-FINE-N---a
common occurrence as newer edge-optimized models are released---cannot assume the
same configuration remains near-optimal and should budget for a re-tuning pass.
In this scenario, the data-curation cost compounds with each new architecture evaluated.

\textit{Conversely, if a consistent optimum emerges} across all three architectures,
it suggests that negative-frame ratio can be treated as a property of the
\emph{dataset}---not the model---at least among lightweight nano-scale detectors.
This would substantially simplify the curation pipeline: teams could determine the
optimal ratio once and apply it across any architecture subsequently evaluated,
decoupling the curation decision from the model selection decision.

\textit{Regarding curation strategy:} if hard-negative mining consistently
outperforms random subsampling across architectures, it validates the additional
engineering effort. If gains are architecture-specific, the curation strategy must
be selected jointly with the architecture. If hard-negative mining yields no
consistent advantage, simple random collection of background frames is sufficient
for all tested architectures---a practically important finding for
resource-constrained AIoT development teams.

\subsection{Deployment Cost Orthogonality}
The deployment-cost framing in Section~\ref{sec:results} highlights that false-positive
rate and inference latency are \emph{orthogonal} levers in AIoT system design.
Latency is fixed by architecture and can only be addressed through model selection,
quantization, or pruning---none of which require training data changes. False-positive
rate is fixed by training-data composition and can only be addressed through retraining
with different negative-frame configurations---regardless of architecture.
This orthogonality means that a system designer cannot trade off latency for
false-positive rate without separately addressing each axis, with direct consequences
for how AIoT teams structure their optimization pipelines.

\subsection{Limitations}
This study evaluates a single dataset and domain. While the 81\% natural negative
prevalence in our driver-monitoring corpus is representative of AIoT streaming
conditions, findings may not transfer to domains with different background statistics,
object density, or positive-class diversity.

We test three architectures at their nano variants only. Larger variants may exhibit
different sensitivity to negative-frame configuration given their greater
representational capacity.

Each experimental condition uses a single training run with one fixed seed, producing
point estimates that preclude formal statistical inference. This is a deliberate compute
budget constraint; we report practical magnitude and trend directionality while
acknowledging that some observed variance may reflect stochastic training dynamics.

The hard-negative mining protocol uses a fixed threshold ($\tau=0.25$) and a single
baseline checkpoint per architecture. More sophisticated strategies (online mining,
iterative refinement, multi-threshold sweeps) remain as natural extensions.

Deployment-cost estimates are derived analytically from FP-per-frame rates rather than
measured on physical edge hardware, and should be interpreted as directional estimates
proportional to FP/1k rather than as validated power or bandwidth measurements.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presents the first cross-architecture empirical study of negative-frame
training configuration for lightweight object detectors in a real-world,
safety-critical AIoT setting. Training YOLO11n, YOLO26n, and D-FINE-N on a
15{,}723-frame driver-monitoring corpus under 21 systematically varied experimental
conditions (five ratio levels plus hard-negative curation per architecture), we provide
the first empirical evidence on whether the performance-maximizing negative-frame
configuration is architecture-invariant or architecture-specific.

By translating accuracy differences into estimated nuisance-alert rates per hour, we
demonstrate that training-data curation choices carry deployment consequences invisible
in standard mAP metrics but directly consequential for user trust, edge compute
efficiency, and uplink bandwidth in continuously streaming AIoT systems---while leaving
inference latency, governed by architecture alone, unaffected.

The practical answer to whether negative-frame curation must be re-tuned when switching
among lightweight detector architectures directly informs how AIoT teams budget for
data curation alongside model selection.

Future work includes: extending the analysis to additional AIoT application domains;
testing findings across model scales within each architecture family; validating
deployment-cost estimates against measured power and bandwidth telemetry on physical
edge hardware; and investigating online and iterative hard-negative mining strategies.
