\title{Negative Frames Are Not Architecture-Agnostic: A Cross-Detector
       Study of Negative-Frame Configuration for Edge AIoT Driver Monitoring}

\begin{abstract}
Edge AIoT object detectors, such as in-cabin driver-monitoring systems,
continuously process video streams in which background-only (negative) frames
dominate the natural operational distribution (approximately 81\% negative
prevalence). While incorporating negative frames during training mitigates false
positives, prior work tunes the negative-to-positive proportion in isolation for
individual detector architectures, leaving open whether an optimally tuned
configuration transfers across architecturally distinct models. This paper presents
the first controlled, cross-architecture empirical investigation of negative-frame
ratio sensitivity and curation quality across three lightweight edge detectors:
YOLO11n (anchor-free CNN), YOLO26n (next-generation CNN), and D-FINE-N
(transformer-based real-time DETR). Evaluating five nested ratio configurations
(0\% to 80\% negative frames) and a hard-negative mining strategy at matched
dataset size on a 15,723-frame driver-monitoring corpus, we determine whether
the performance-maximizing negative-frame ratio is architecture-invariant or
architecture-specific. We further translate false-positive rates per 1,000 frames
into estimated nuisance-alert rates per hour, demonstrating that training-data
curation directly governs edge deployment usability independently of model
inference latency.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Object detectors deployed in AIoT systems---such as in-cabin driver monitoring,
smart surveillance, and industrial defect inspection---operate under a condition
that standard computer-vision benchmarks rarely reflect: the vast majority of
frames encountered at inference time contain \emph{no} target object.
A driver-monitoring camera streams continuously for hours per trip, yet the events
it must flag (distraction, drowsiness, unsafe behavior) occupy only a small fraction
of that stream.
This asymmetry means a detector's behavior on background-only frames---rather than
its accuracy on object-dense benchmark images---ultimately determines whether the
system is usable in the field.
A model prone to false positives on ordinary footage produces nuisance alerts that
erode user trust, waste edge compute cycles, and, in bandwidth- or
battery-constrained AIoT deployments, trigger unnecessary uplink transmissions or
actuator responses.

The standard mitigation is to include negative (background-only) frames during
training, and prior work confirms this lever is powerful.
Reported optimal negative-frame proportions range from single
digits~\cite{b8} to over 60\% of the training set in safety-critical driving
contexts~\cite{b6}, with performance following a non-monotonic, inverted-U
relationship: too few negatives leave false positives unaddressed, while too many
suppress recall by pushing the detector toward over-conservative predictions~\cite{b5}.
However, every empirical study we are aware of tunes this parameter for a
\emph{single} detector architecture in isolation.
It remains unknown whether a negative-frame configuration---including ratio or the
choice between random and hard-mined negatives---tuned for one architecture
transfers to another, or whether the optimum is itself architecture-dependent.

This is not a minor gap.
AIoT practitioners routinely choose among CNN-based, anchor-free, and
transformer-based lightweight detectors under a shared, fixed data-curation budget.
If the answer to ``how many negative frames do I need'' changes with the architecture
choice, then architecture selection and data-curation decisions cannot be made
independently---as much current practice implicitly assumes.

This paper addresses that gap through a controlled, cross-architecture study of
negative-frame configuration on a real-world, safety-critical AIoT dataset.
Using an in-cabin driver-monitoring corpus with a naturally occurring 19:81
positive-to-negative frame ratio, we train three lightweight detectors spanning
distinct design paradigms (YOLO11n, YOLO26n, and D-FINE-N) under systematically
varied negative-frame ratios and sampling strategies, holding all other training
factors fixed. We ask:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1:} Does the negative-frame ratio that maximizes detection
        performance differ across detector architectures, or does a single
        configuration generalize?
  \item \textbf{RQ2:} At each architecture's best ratio, does replacing randomly
        sampled negatives with hard-mined negatives (frames on which a baseline
        model produces false positives) yield further, architecture-dependent gains?
\end{itemize}

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Cross-architecture negative-frame benchmark.} The first empirical
        sweep of negative-frame ratio sensitivity across three lightweight detectors
        spanning CNN and transformer paradigms, on a real, safety-critical AIoT
        dataset (in-cabin driver monitoring).
  \item \textbf{Hard-negative curation protocol and cost-benefit analysis.} A
        mining strategy targeting false-positive-prone background frames, with a
        controlled comparison against random sampling at matched dataset size.
  \item \textbf{Operational deployment-cost reframing.} A translation of FP-per-frame
        differences into nuisance-alert rates per hour, connecting an accuracy metric
        to a deployment consequence AIoT system designers plan around, while explicitly
        separating inference latency (architecture-bound) from FP rate (training-data-bound).
\end{enumerate}
\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Class Imbalance and Negative Sampling in Object Detection}
Negative sampling has long been recognized as a first-order design choice in
object-detector training. At the loss level, Focal Loss~\cite{b1} was introduced
because the vast number of easy negatives in dense-prediction detectors overwhelms
training signals from the rare foreground class; it addresses imbalance through
adaptive loss reweighting rather than through the ratio of negative instances itself.
Related work shows that negatives are not static targets during training: they can
drift toward foreground appearance as training proceeds, confusing the detector's
foreground-background boundary and motivating dynamic re-selection~\cite{b2}.

In anchor-based versus anchor-free comparisons, the rule used to assign positive
and negative training samples has been shown to have a larger effect on final
performance than the underlying regression formulation~\cite{b3}, reinforcing that
sample assignment---rather than architecture alone---is a primary driver of
detection behavior. At the region level, mining strategies that surface visually
confusable background chips measurably raise mAP relative to training on
positive-only regions~\cite{b4}.

\subsection{Negative-Frame Ratio as a Dataset-Curation Lever}
Standard guidance for one-stage YOLO detectors recommends including roughly 0--10\%
background-only images to reduce false positives~\cite{b8}, without cross-architecture
validation. Direct empirical sweeps reveal a non-monotonic relationship: one study
varying the proportion from 0\% to 40\% across two YOLO variants found that recall
and mAP@50 peaked near 20\% before declining, with excess negatives causing
over-conservative predictions~\cite{b5}. In a safety-critical driving context, a
far higher proportion was needed: raising background images from 10\% to over 60\%
cut false positives by more than 80\% while also reducing false negatives by
25\%~\cite{b6}, suggesting the effective operating point is strongly domain-dependent.
In human--object interaction detection, scaling the negative-to-positive pair ratio
from roughly 10:1 to 1000:1 produced a large, consistent performance gain~\cite{b7},
underscoring how strongly the ``correct'' configuration depends on task structure.

\subsection{Research Gap}
Taken together, this literature establishes that negative-sample configuration
reliably shifts a detector's precision-recall operating point, and that the effective
optimum varies dramatically across tasks and domains. However, in every case reviewed,
the evaluation is conducted on a \emph{single} detector architecture at a time. No
existing study holds negative-frame configuration fixed while \emph{varying} detector
architecture, leaving open the key question of whether a ratio or curation strategy
tuned for one model transfers to another. This is precisely the question AIoT
practitioners face when choosing among architecturally distinct lightweight detectors
under a shared data budget---a gap our work fills directly.
