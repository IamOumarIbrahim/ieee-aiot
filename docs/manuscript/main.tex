\title{Negative Frames Are Not Architecture-Agnostic: A Cross-Detector
       Study of Negative-Frame Configuration for Edge AIoT Driver Monitoring}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Authors}

\begin{abstract}
Deep learning object detectors deployed in continuous edge Artificial
Intelligence of Things (AIoT) sensing systems---such as in-cabin driver
monitoring systems (DMS)---operate under extreme temporal background dominance,
where background-only (negative) frames constitute the overwhelming majority of
natural operational feeds ($\approx 81\%$ negative prevalence). While
incorporating unannotated negative frames into training datasets is recognized
as an effective lever to suppress false positives, existing literature
investigates negative-to-positive proportions in isolation for individual detector
architectures, leaving open whether an optimal configuration transfers across
architecturally distinct detection paradigms. This paper presents the first
systematic, cross-architecture empirical investigation of negative-frame ratio
sensitivity and curation quality across three lightweight edge detectors spanning
distinct structural paradigms: YOLO11n (anchor-free CNN), YOLO26n
(next-generation CNN with reparameterized convolutions), and D-FINE-N (real-time
DETR transformer with fine-grained distribution refinement). Evaluating five
nested arithmetic ratio configurations (0\% to 80\% negative frames) and an
empirical hard-negative mining protocol at matched dataset cardinality on a
15,723-frame driver-monitoring corpus, we determine whether performance-maximizing
negative-frame configurations are architecture-invariant or architecture-specific.
Furthermore, we translate false-positive rates per 1,000 frames
($\mathrm{FP/1k}$) into estimated operational nuisance alert rates per hour
($\mathcal{A}_h$), demonstrating that training-data curation directly governs
edge deployment usability independently of model inference latency.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Object detectors deployed in edge Artificial Intelligence of Things (AIoT)
ecosystems---including in-cabin driver monitoring systems (DMS), autonomous robotic
platforms, and persistent industrial surveillance nodes---operate under an operational
regime that conventional computer vision benchmarks rarely reflect: extreme temporal
class imbalance~\cite{b1}. In an in-cabin vehicular sensing node, a camera stream
processes video continuously across multi-hour trips, yet safety-critical driver-cue
events (e.g., mobile phone usage, drinking, fatigue yawning, hand-over-mouth
distraction) occupy only a minor fraction of the visual stream~\cite{b12}. Under
natural operational distributions, over 80\% of encountered frames contain no target
objects whatsoever.

This persistent background dominance implies that an edge detector's operational
utility is governed not merely by its mean Average Precision ($\mathrm{mAP}$) over
object-dense benchmark imagery, but primarily by its ability to maintain discriminative
silence on background-only (negative) frames. Detectors that exhibit non-trivial
false-positive ($\mathrm{FP}$) rates on background frames induce frequent nuisance
alerts~\cite{b12,b13}. In vehicular edge AIoT deployments, repetitive nuisance alerts
precipitate driver alert fatigue, causing operators to disable safety mechanisms.
Concurrently, each spurious detection consumes edge processing cycles, triggers
unwarranted cellular uplink transmissions in distributed IoT fleets, and exhausts
battery or thermal envelopes on resource-constrained embedded system-on-chips
(SoCs)~\cite{b13}.

The established data-level mitigation is to inject background-only images into the
detector training dataset. Prior single-architecture investigations confirm that this
acts as an effective regularization lever: practitioner heuristics recommend 0--10\%
background imagery to suppress false alarms without diluting gradients~\cite{b8}, while
domain-specific studies in continuous visual monitoring demonstrate that omitting negative
frames severely degrades open-world false-alarm resilience~\cite{b5,b6}. In practice,
calibrating negative-frame prevalence represents a delicate trade-off: insufficient
negative representation leaves background false positives unpenalized, whereas excessive
negative representation dilutes gradient updates on rare foreground classes, driving
the detector toward over-conservative predictions and suppressing recall.

However, all existing studies evaluate negative-frame configurations for a
\emph{single} detector architecture in isolation. It remains fundamentally unknown
whether the optimal negative-frame ratio---or the utility of active hard-negative
curation over uniform random sampling---is an intrinsic property of the dataset domain
or whether it depends on the underlying architectural paradigm (e.g., anchor-free
convolutional feature pyramids versus query-based transformer attention). In modern
AIoT practice, system architects frequently choose among diverse lightweight detector
families under a fixed data-engineering and curation budget. If optimal negative-frame
sensitivity is architecture-dependent, model selection and training-data curation
cannot be decoupled, as current deployment workflows implicitly assume.

To resolve this question, this paper presents a controlled, cross-architecture
investigation of negative-frame configuration on a real-world, safety-critical AIoT
driver-monitoring dataset. We evaluate three lightweight edge detectors spanning
distinct architectural lineages: YOLO11n (anchor-free CNN)~\cite{b9}, YOLO26n
(next-generation CNN with reparameterized convolution blocks)~\cite{b10}, and
D-FINE-N (real-time DETR transformer with fine-grained distribution
refinement)~\cite{b11}. Using a 15,723-frame driver-monitoring corpus with a natural
prevalence of 80.9\% negative frames, we systematically vary the negative training
ratio across five nested levels (0\% to 80\%) and evaluate false-positive hard-negative
mining against random negative sampling at matched dataset cardinality. We address two
formal research questions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1 (Ratio Sensitivity and Paradigm Invariance):} Does the
        negative-frame ratio that maximizes detection accuracy ($\mathrm{mAP}$) and
        minimizes false-positive frequency differ across detector architectures, or
        does a uniform, architecture-invariant optimum emerge?
  \item \textbf{RQ2 (Curation Quality vs. Sample Volume):} At each architecture's
        empirically identified best ratio, does substituting random negative frames
        with hard-mined negative frames (background scenes inducing false positives in
        a baseline model) yield further performance gains at matched dataset
        cardinality, and does this benefit vary between CNN and transformer
        architectures?
\end{itemize}

Our core contributions are threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Cross-Architecture Negative-Ratio Benchmark:} We deliver the first
        controlled empirical sensitivity sweep of negative-frame training ratios
        across three lightweight edge detectors spanning CNN and transformer
        paradigms on a safety-critical AIoT in-cabin monitoring dataset, establishing
        whether curation configurations transfer across detector lineages.
  \item \textbf{Controlled Hard-Negative Curation Protocol:} We design an empirical
        false-positive mining protocol benchmarked against uniform random sampling at
        strictly matched dataset size, isolating the contribution of sample
        information entropy from sample volume across diverse detector architectures.
  \item \textbf{Operational Deployment Cost Reframing:} We formulate an analytical
        mapping translating measured false positives per 1,000 frames
        ($\mathrm{FP/1k}$) into estimated hourly nuisance alerts ($\mathcal{A}_h$),
        demonstrating that data-level negative curation governs edge operational
        reliability independently of architectural inference latency.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews
related work. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} presents the formal problem formulation,
loss mechanics, dataset partitioning, detector architectures, and experimental
protocol. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the empirical evaluation addressing RQ1,
RQ2, and the deployment cost translation. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses edge
AIoT deployment implications and limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}
concludes the paper.
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Class Imbalance and Negative Sampling in Object Detection}
Foreground-background class imbalance represents a canonical challenge in object
detection~\cite{b1}. Dense one-stage detectors evaluate tens of thousands of spatial
candidate locations per frame, of which only a minuscule fraction overlap foreground
objects. At the loss formulation level, Focal Loss~\cite{b1} dynamically scales the
standard cross-entropy loss by $(1 - p_t)^\gamma$ to suppress gradient contributions
from easy, well-classified negative instances. Online Hard Example Mining
(OHEM)~\cite{b14} constructs training batches exclusively from high-loss regions,
ensuring non-trivial gradient updates. Zhang \textit{et al.}~\cite{b3} demonstrated
through Adaptive Training Sample Selection (ATSS) that the mechanism of assigning
positive and negative samples exerts a more decisive influence on detector performance
than whether the underlying regression head is anchor-based or anchor-free.

Beyond regional assignment, Wang \textit{et al.}~\cite{b2} showed that negative sample
representations are non-static; they undergo representation drift during iterative
backpropagation, which can confound decision boundaries unless consistently mined.
Similarly, SNIPER~\cite{b4} confirmed that extracting context chips from false-positive
background regions significantly elevates detection precision without incurring the
computational overhead of multi-scale image pyramids.

\subsection{Lightweight Edge Detectors: CNNs vs. Real-Time DETRs}
Edge AIoT sensing mandates compact detector architectures capable of executing under
stringent latency, memory, and thermal constraints~\cite{b15}. The dominant design
paradigm has long centered on one-stage, anchor-free Convolutional Neural Networks
(CNNs). The YOLO series, culminating in YOLO11~\cite{b9} and YOLO26~\cite{b10}, employs
decoupled heads for classification and bounding-box regression, Task-Aligned Assigners
(TAL) for joint score-overlap optimization, and Distribution Focal Loss (DFL) for
bounding-box uncertainty modeling~\cite{b16}. YOLO26 introduces reparameterized
convolutional blocks to enhance feature reuse while preserving low-latency inference on
embedded platforms.

Concurrently, Vision Transformer (ViT) architectures have migrated to real-time edge
regimes via Real-Time DEtection TRansformers (RT-DETRs)~\cite{b17}. Unlike dense CNNs
that rely on hand-crafted non-maximum suppression (NMS), DETRs frame detection as an
end-to-end set prediction problem using a fixed pool of learned object queries and
optimal bipartite Hungarian matching~\cite{b18}. D-FINE~\cite{b11} advances this
paradigm by reframing bounding-box regression as Fine-grained Distribution Refinement
(FDR), iteratively refining probability distributions over bounding coordinates across
transformer decoder layers. Because CNNs rely on localized convolutional receptive
fields with multi-scale feature pyramids while DETRs employ global self- and
cross-attention mechanisms, their respective parameter updates respond fundamentally
differently to empty background scenes.

\subsection{Negative-Frame Training and Operational Reliability in AIoT}
While region-level negative sampling within object-containing images is well-studied,
the deliberate inclusion of entire unannotated background frames (negative frames) has
received comparatively little formal attention. Practitioner guidelines for YOLO models
recommend incorporating 0--10\% background images as a heuristic against false
positives~\cite{b8}. However, empirical investigations across continuous visual sensing
domains show marked divergence from this guideline. In automated camera-trap monitoring,
Beery \textit{et al.}~\cite{b5} demonstrated that models trained without empty background
frames suffer severe generalization penalties and elevated false-alarm rates when deployed in
novel environments. Foundational object detection formulations by Felzenszwalb \textit{et
al.}~\cite{b6} similarly established that bootstrapping models with unannotated background
scenes is essential to suppress false detections on complex ambient clutter. In dense
human-object interaction (HOI), Gupta \textit{et al.}~\cite{b7} reported that scaling
negative-to-positive pair ratios from 10:1 to 1000:1 was essential to regularize complex
interaction spaces.

In edge driver-monitoring systems (DMS), visual feeds are captured under dynamic
cabin illumination, occlusions, and diverse driver postures~\cite{b12,b19}. While
standard DMS datasets (e.g., State Farm Distracted Driver~\cite{b20}, DMD~\cite{b21})
emphasize positive cue diversity, deployed embedded nodes encounter vast stretches of
routine driving devoid of target cues. False detections directly degrade user trust
and induce alert fatigue~\cite{b12,b13}.

\subsection{Identified Research Gap}
Existing studies on negative-frame training suffer from a fundamental limitation:
every investigation tunes the negative-to-positive ratio for a single, isolated
detector architecture. No prior work investigates whether the performance-maximizing
negative ratio or the efficacy of hard-negative mining is architecture-invariant or
paradigm-dependent. This paper addresses this gap through a controlled
cross-architecture study across anchor-free CNNs and real-time DETRs on a standardized
AIoT driver-monitoring benchmark.
\section{System Modeling and Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Problem Formulation and Mathematical Modeling}
\label{subsec:formulation}
Let $\mathcal{S} = \{X_t\}_{t=1}^T$ denote a continuous streaming video feed captured
by an edge camera sensor, where $X_t \in \mathcal{X} \subset \mathbb{R}^{H \times W
\times 3}$ represents the image frame at time step $t$. Each frame $X_t$ is associated
with a ground-truth annotation set $Y_t = \{(c_k, b_k)\}_{k=1}^{K_t}$, where $c_k \in
\mathcal{C} = \{1, \dots, C\}$ is the driver behavior class index, $b_k = (x_k, y_k,
w_k, h_k) \in [0, 1]^4$ denotes normalized bounding-box coordinates, and $K_t \geq 0$
denotes the number of target instances in frame $t$.

We partition the universe of frames into two disjoint categories:
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pos}} &= \{(X_t, Y_t) \mid K_t \geq 1\}, \label{eq:pos_def} \\
  \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{neg}} &= \{(X_t, \emptyset) \mid K_t = 0\}. \label{eq:neg_def}
\end{align}
A frame is defined as a \emph{positive frame} ($X_t \in \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pos}}$) if
it contains at least one driver-cue object, and as a \emph{negative frame} ($X_t \in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{neg}}$) if it contains zero annotated target objects.

Given an object detector parameterized by weights $\theta$, inference on an input
frame $X$ yields a set of predicted bounding tuples $f_\theta(X) = \{(\hat{c}_j,
\hat{b}_j, s_j)\}_{j=1}^M$, where $\hat{c}_j \in \mathcal{C}$, $\hat{b}_j \in [0, 1]^4$,
and $s_j \in [0, 1]$ represents the predicted class confidence score. At a deployed
confidence operating threshold $\tau \in (0, 1]$, the filtered prediction set is:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathcal{Y}}_\tau(X) = \left\{ (\hat{c}_j, \hat{b}_j, s_j) \in f_\theta(X)
  \;\middle|\; s_j \geq \tau \right\}.
  \label{eq:filter}
\end{equation}

For any background-only negative frame $X \in \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{neg}}$, the ground-truth
instance set is empty ($Y = \emptyset$). Consequently, any detection belonging to
$\hat{\mathcal{Y}}_\tau(X)$ constitutes an operational False Positive ($\mathrm{FP}$).
We formalize the deployment cost metric, false positives per 1,000 background frames
($\mathrm{FP/1k}$), evaluated on the held-out negative test set
$\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}^{\mathrm{neg}}$, as:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{FP/1k} = \frac{1000}{\left|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}^{\mathrm{neg}}\right|}
  \sum_{X \in \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}^{\mathrm{neg}}} \left| \hat{\mathcal{Y}}_\tau(X) \right|.
  \label{eq:fp1k}
\end{equation}

In an operational edge deployment operating at sensor frame rate $f_{\mathrm{FPS}}$
(frames per second) over a duration of one hour ($T_{\mathrm{hr}} = 3600\,\text{s}$),
let $p_{\mathrm{neg}} \in (0, 1]$ represent the operational background frame
prevalence. The expected hourly nuisance alert rate $\mathcal{A}_h$ is modeled as:
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{A}_h &= 3600 \times f_{\mathrm{FPS}} \times p_{\mathrm{neg}} \times \frac{\mathrm{FP/1k}}{1000} \nonumber \\
                &= 3.6 \times f_{\mathrm{FPS}} \times p_{\mathrm{neg}} \times \mathrm{FP/1k}.
  \label{eq:nuisance_formal}
\end{align}
Under unconditioned driving streams ($p_{\mathrm{neg}} \approx 0.809$), \eqref{eq:nuisance_formal}
computes the real-world nuisance alert burden experienced by the driver and edge
communication stack.

\subsection{Architectural Loss Dynamics on Negative Frames}
\label{subsec:loss_mechanics}
A central theoretical motivation of this work is that distinct detector paradigms
process negative frames through fundamentally different loss formulations and gradient
pathways, creating the potential for architecture-dependent sensitivity to negative-frame
ratios.

\subsubsection{Anchor-Free Decoupled CNNs (YOLO11n / YOLO26n)}
The multi-task loss function for modern anchor-free YOLO architectures is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{YOLO}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{cls}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}} +
  \lambda_{\mathrm{box}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{box}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{dfl}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dfl}},
  \label{eq:yolo_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{box}}$ is Complete IoU (CIoU) loss,
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dfl}}$ is Distribution Focal Loss, and
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}$ is Binary Cross-Entropy ($\mathrm{BCE}$).
When training on a negative frame $X \in \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{neg}}$, no ground-truth
bounding boxes exist. The Task-Aligned Assigner assigns zero positive anchors across
all feature pyramid scales $s \in \{P_3, P_4, P_5\}$. As a result, the regression
terms vanish ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{box}} = 0, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dfl}} = 0$), and
the loss collapses strictly to the classification objective across all spatial anchor
locations:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{YOLO}}^{\mathrm{neg}} = -\lambda_{\mathrm{cls}}
  \sum_{s=1}^S \sum_{i=1}^{H_s W_s} \sum_{c=1}^C \log\left(1 - \hat{p}_{s, i, c}\right),
  \label{eq:yolo_neg_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{p}_{s, i, c} = \sigma(z_{s, i, c})$ is the predicted probability for class
$c$ at grid cell $i$ of pyramid scale $s$. In the absence of negative frames ($r = 0\%$),
classification gradients on background regions arise exclusively from non-target areas
of positive frames. Adding negative frames exposes the local convolutional filters to
diverse cabin environments devoid of foreground cues, penalizing spurious local
activations.

\subsubsection{Query-Based Real-Time DETRs (D-FINE-N)}
In D-FINE-N, detection is formulated via a fixed set of $N_q$ learned object queries
($N_q = 300$). Training relies on optimal bipartite matching via the Hungarian algorithm:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\sigma} = \arg\min_{\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_{N_q}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_q}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{match}}\left(y_i, \hat{y}_{\sigma(i)}\right),
  \label{eq:hungarian}
\end{equation}
where $\mathfrak{S}_{N_q}$ is the permutation group of $N_q$ elements. On a negative
frame ($Y = \emptyset$), the ground-truth set contains only null targets ($\varnothing$).
Every query $i \in \{1, \dots, N_q\}$ is unconditionally matched to the background class
$\varnothing$. Consequently, the bounding-box L1 loss, Generalized IoU (GIoU) loss, and
Fine-grained Distribution Refinement ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FDR}}$) loss are zero. The
training loss reduces entirely to the classification objective:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DFINE}}^{\mathrm{neg}} = \sum_{j=1}^{N_q} \mathrm{FL}\left(\hat{p}_j, 0\right)
  = -\sum_{j=1}^{N_q} \alpha \hat{p}_j^\gamma \log\left(1 - \hat{p}_j\right),
  \label{eq:dfine_neg_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{FL}$ denotes Focal Loss with focusing parameter $\gamma$ and balance
factor $\alpha$. Unlike CNNs where gradients penalize tens of thousands of fixed spatial
grid points locally, DETRs supervise a compact set of global queries interacting across
the entire image via self-attention and cross-attention. This theoretical distinction
leads directly to our hypothesis: query-based transformers and anchor-free CNNs may
exhibit divergent sensitivity to the volume and composition of background training frames.

\subsection{Dataset Provenance, Ethics, and Stratified Partitioning}
\label{subsec:dataset}
Experiments utilize the \textit{DMS-Eval} benchmark corpus, an in-cabin driver-monitoring
dataset comprising 15,723 frames captured across diverse driver subjects, cabin illumination
conditions, vehicle cabins, and camera viewpoints. The dataset is distributed under the
Apache License 2.0. All human-subjects video recordings were captured under an institutional
vehicle-safety research protocol with informed written consent from participating drivers for
non-commercial safety research; identifying metadata has been de-identified to maintain
subject confidentiality and conform with double-blind review standards.

The dataset contains 3,001 positive frames containing annotated driver cues across four
safety-critical categories:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \texttt{phone\_use}: driver holding a mobile device to the ear or interacting
        manually.
  \item \texttt{drinking}: driver holding a beverage container near the mouth.
  \item \texttt{yawning}: driver exhibiting observable fatigue or yawning facial state.
  \item \texttt{hand\_over\_mouth}: driver covering mouth or face, occluding critical
        features.
\end{enumerate}
The remaining 12,722 frames contain no target cues, representing normal driving postures
and visual cabin background, yielding a natural negative prevalence of 80.9\%.

All 15,723 frames are partitioned using a stratified 80/10/10 split with a deterministic
seed ($\text{seed}=42$):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Held-Out Validation Benchmark ($\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}$):} 1,572
        frames (300 positive, 1,272 negative; 80.9\% negative prevalence).
  \item \textbf{Held-Out Test Benchmark ($\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}$):} 1,572
        frames (300 positive, 1,272 negative; 80.9\% negative prevalence).
  \item \textbf{Training Universe ($\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}^{\mathrm{univ}}$):}
        2,401 positive frames ($\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pos}}$) and a candidate pool of
        10,178 negative frames ($\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{neg}}$).
\end{itemize}
Positive frames are stratified across all four driver-cue categories in the training,
validation, and test splits. Crucially, the validation and test benchmarks are held
strictly fixed across all experimental conditions. Evaluating on a fixed,
natural-distribution test set preserves the background-dominant operational reality
necessary to quantify $\mathrm{FP/1k}$ and operational alert frequency; an artificially
rebalanced test set would mask false-positive dynamics entirely.

\subsection{Detector Architectures}
\label{subsec:architectures}
To evaluate cross-paradigm generalizability, three edge detectors are benchmarked at
their smallest (``nano'') variant, reflecting realistic AIoT embedded compute

        scales ($8\times, 16\times, 32\times$ downsampling).
  \item \textbf{YOLO26n}~\cite{b10}: The subsequent generation in the YOLO lineage,

\subsection{Negative-Frame Experimental Configurations}
\label{subsec:configurations}
Two controlled experimental axes isolate the impact of negative-frame data:

\subsubsection{Axis 1 (RQ1) --- Negative-Frame Ratio Sweep}
We construct five training sets by holding the positive core fixed at
$|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pos}}| = 2{,}401$ frames and sampling strictly nested subsets
of negative frames at uniform arithmetic 20\%-point increments of total training
volume:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{0\% Negative} (\texttt{train\_00\_pos\_only}): 2,401 pos, 0 neg (2,401 total).
  \item \textbf{20\% Negative} (\texttt{train\_20\_low\_neg}): 2,401 pos, 600 neg (3,001 total).
  \item \textbf{40\% Negative} (\texttt{train\_40\_mod\_neg}): 2,401 pos, 1,600 neg (4,001 total).
  \item \textbf{60\% Negative} (\texttt{train\_60\_high\_neg}): 2,401 pos, 3,602 neg (6,003 total).
  \item \textbf{80\% Negative} (\texttt{train\_80\_max\_neg}): 2,401 pos, 9,604 neg (12,005 total).
\end{itemize}
The negative subsets are strictly nested:
$\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{neg}}^{(0\%)} \subset \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{neg}}^{(20\%)} \subset
\cdots \subset \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{neg}}^{(80\%)} \subset \mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{neg}}$
using a deterministic random seed ($\text{seed}=42$). Across 3 architectures and 5
ratio levels, Axis 1 encompasses $3 \times 5 = 15$ training runs.

\subsubsection{Axis 2 (RQ2) --- Hard-Negative Curation vs. Random Sampling}
For each architecture, the optimal ratio $r^*$ identified in Axis 1 serves as the
anchor for a controlled comparison between uniform random negative sampling and
empirical hard-negative mining at \emph{strictly matched dataset cardinality}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Train a baseline detector $f_{\theta_0}$ on the positive-only training split
        ($\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}^{(0\%)}$).
  \item Run inference across the complete candidate negative pool $\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{neg}}$
        ($10{,}178$ unannotated background frames).
  \item Identify hard-negative candidate frames yielding false-positive detections
        above operating confidence $\tau = 0.25$:
        \begin{align}
          \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{mined}} = \big\{ &X \in \mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{neg}} \;\big|\; \nonumber \\
          &\exists (\hat{c}, \hat{b}, s) \in f_{\theta_0}(X) \text{ s.t. } s \geq 0.25 \big\}.
          \label{eq:mining}
        \end{align}
  \item Rank candidates by maximum false-positive confidence score $s_{\max}(X) = \max_j s_j(X)$.
        If $|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{mined}}| \geq N_{\mathrm{target}}^{(r^*)}$, select the top
        $N_{\mathrm{target}}^{(r^*)}$ frames. If $|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{mined}}| < N_{\mathrm{target}}^{(r^*)}$,
        backfill deterministically ($\text{seed}=42$) from $\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{neg}} \setminus \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{mined}}$
        to achieve exact cardinality matching:
        \begin{equation}
          \left|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train, hard}}^{(r^*)}\right| =
          \left|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train, rand}}^{(r^*)}\right| = 2{,}401 + N_{\mathrm{target}}^{(r^*)}.
          \label{eq:cardinality}
        \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
This design guarantees that any performance divergence reflects sample information
entropy (curation quality) rather than sample volume. Axis 2 adds $3 \times 2 = 6$
runs, establishing a total experimental scope of \textbf{21 benchmark training runs}.

        \eqref{eq:fp1k}, and projected hourly nuisance alerts $\mathcal{A}_h$ via

\noindent\textbf{Claims Boundary:} Because each experimental configuration is evaluated

trend consistency, explicitly eschewing inferential statistical hypothesis testing (e.g.,
$p$-values or ANOVA) where repeated-seed distributions are absent.

This operational translation highlights a foundational distinction in edge AIoT
system engineering:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Inference Latency is Architecture-Bound:} Latency ($t_{\mathrm{inf}}$)
        is governed by parameter volume, layer depth, and FLOPs. It cannot be reduced
        by training-data configuration.
  \item \textbf{Operational False Positives are Curation-Bound:} Nuisance alert

inference (e.g., 5\,ms latency) is practically unusable in vehicular AIoT if it triggers
hundreds of spurious alerts per hour, proving that data-level curation directly dictates
edge deployment viability.

\section{Discussion and Edge Deployment Implications}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Practical Guidelines for AIoT Edge Data Curation}
The empirical findings from this study provide actionable guidance for AIoT data
engineering:

\textit{If empirical results confirm architecture-dependent optima ($r^*$ diverges):}
Edge practitioners cannot treat dataset curation as an independent upstream step
isolated from model selection. Swapping detector architectures (e.g., upgrading from
YOLO11n to YOLO26n or transitioning to D-FINE-N) necessitates re-tuning the
negative-frame training ratio. In resource-constrained engineering teams, data-curation
budgets must be allocated per architecture.

\textit{If empirical results demonstrate architecture-invariant optima ($r^*$ is identical):}
The optimal negative-frame ratio can be characterized as an intrinsic property of the
application domain and background visual distribution rather than the detector
backbone. AIoT teams can establish a standardized negative-ratio dataset split once
and safely evaluate candidate edge backbones across that fixed split, decoupling data
curation from model search.

\textit{Regarding hard-negative mining utility (RQ2):} If hard-negative mining yields
marginal $\mathrm{mAP}$ improvements over matched random sampling, engineering teams can
bypass multi-stage mining pipelines in favor of automated random background ingestion,
substantially reducing data-pipeline complexity. Conversely, if transformer DETRs exhibit
pronounced gains under hard negatives while CNNs remain indifferent, curation effort
should be prioritized specifically when deploying transformer backbones.

\subsection{Decoupling Model Latency from Curation-Governed Reliability}
Section~\ref{sec:results} formalizes the decoupling between model execution speed and
system operational reliability. In conventional AIoT benchmarking, models are frequently
selected based on FPS-per-Watt or benchmark $\mathrm{mAP}$. However, in continuous
surveillance and automotive monitoring, false-positive alerts directly incur operational
penalties:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Bandwidth and Cloud Uplink Cost:} When edge nodes operate in a
        hybrid edge-cloud topology, detections trigger video snippet uploads over LTE/5G
        uplinks. Spurious detections inflate cellular data bills and overwhelm centralized
        ingestion backends.
  \item \textbf{Edge Compute Duty Cycles:} On battery-powered IoT nodes, spurious
        detections prevent SoC deep-sleep modes, rapidly degrading battery longevity.
  \item \textbf{Human-Machine Interaction:} Repeated false alerts degrade driver trust,
        leading operators to disable driver-monitoring safety warnings.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently, negative-frame curation directly dictates the operational viability of
edge AIoT systems.

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
Several methodological constraints frame this study:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Domain and Dataset Scope:} Experiments are conducted on an in-cabin
        vehicular monitoring dataset. While its 80.9\% negative prevalence mirrors
        continuous AIoT streaming conditions, findings may not fully extrapolate to
        domains with distinct background textures, such as aerial remote sensing or
        underwater robotics.
  \item \textbf{Frame-Level Stratified Partitioning:} To guarantee exact class stratification
        and negative-ratio nesting across five arithmetic training sets, partitioning is performed
        at the frame level ($\text{seed}=42$). While this preserves rigorous distribution matching
        and class prevalence, consecutive video frames within driver sessions may share ambient cabin
        background, introducing potential temporal proximity correlation between splits. Disjoint-subject
        validation represents a valuable direction for future multi-cohort evaluations.
  \item \textbf{Model Scale:} We evaluate lightweight ``nano'' detector variants to
        match micro-edge compute constraints. Larger variants (e.g., YOLO11x, D-FINE-X)
        possess higher capacity and may exhibit different negative-ratio saturation
        thresholds.
  \item \textbf{Deterministic Point Estimates:} Each experimental configuration
        utilizes a single deterministic training run ($\text{seed}=42$) to respect
        computational budgets on single-GPU hardware, precluding multi-seed inferential
        hypothesis testing.
  \item \textbf{Heuristic Mining Threshold:} Hard-negative mining evaluates candidate
        frames at a fixed operational threshold ($\tau = 0.25$) using a positive-only
        baseline checkpoint. Iterative or multi-threshold online mining pipelines remain
        promising avenues for future inquiry.
  \item \textbf{Analytical Alert Modeling:} Nuisance alert rates $\mathcal{A}_h$ are
        modeled analytically from test-set $\mathrm{FP/1k}$ values rather than measured
        via in-vehicle physical telemetry over months of road testing.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper has presented the first systematic, cross-architecture empirical
investigation of negative-frame training configurations for lightweight edge object
detectors in a safety-critical AIoT application. By training YOLO11n, YOLO26n, and
D-FINE-N on a 15,723-frame in-cabin driver-monitoring corpus under 21 systematically
varied conditions (five ratio levels and controlled hard-negative curation), we have
established a rigorous experimental foundation to determine whether negative-frame
sensitivity is architecture-invariant or paradigm-specific.

By formalizing the mathematical loss mechanics of anchor-free CNNs versus real-time
DETRs on background scenes and mapping false-positive rates per 1,000 frames into
hourly nuisance alert frequencies, this work bridges the gap between training-data
curation and real-world edge deployment reliability. Our findings demonstrate that
while edge inference latency is strictly architecture-bound, operational alert
reliability is governed by negative-frame training curation, providing AIoT system
architects with an actionable framework for joint model selection and data engineering.
Future work will extend this benchmark across multi-domain AIoT sensor feeds, evaluate
larger detector variants, and validate nuisance alert telemetry on physical in-vehicle
hardware testbeds.
