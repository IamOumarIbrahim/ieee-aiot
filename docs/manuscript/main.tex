\begin{abstract}
Deep learning object detectors deployed in continuous edge Artificial
Intelligence of Things (AIoT) sensing systems (such as in-cabin driver
monitoring systems) operate under extreme temporal background dominance:
unannotated background-only frames constitute the vast majority of natural
operational feeds ($\approx 81\%$ negative prevalence). While incorporating
negative frames into training suppresses false alarms, existing literature
evaluates negative-to-positive proportions for individual detector
architectures in isolation. It remains unresolved whether an optimal
negative-frame configuration transfers across architecturally distinct
detection designs. This paper presents the first systematic, cross-architecture
empirical study of negative-frame ratio sensitivity across three lightweight edge
detectors with matched capacity ($\approx 2.6\text{M}$ parameters): YOLO11n
(convolutional pyramid with partial self-attention), YOLO26n (pure
reparameterized convolution), and YOLO12n (area-attention vision detector).
Evaluating five nested ratio configurations (0\% to 80\% negatives) and a
hard-negative curation protocol on a 15,723-frame in-cabin driver-monitoring
corpus, we establish that performance-maximizing negative ratios exhibit
architecture-specific dynamics. YOLO11n and YOLO26n both reach peak
$\mathrm{mAP}_{50}$ at a 40\% negative ratio ($0.9932$ and $0.9830$), yet
YOLO26n achieves an $18\times$ false-positive reduction ($5.50\,\mathrm{FP/1k}$)
compared to YOLO11n's $2.5\times$ reduction ($24.37\,\mathrm{FP/1k}$). We
translate false positives per 1,000 frames into estimated operational nuisance
alerts per hour ($\mathcal{A}_h$), demonstrating that training-data curation
governs edge deployment viability independently of raw inference latency.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Object detectors deployed in edge AIoT ecosystems (including in-cabin driver
monitoring systems, robotic platforms, and persistent visual surveillance
nodes) operate under extreme temporal class imbalance~\cite{b1}. In vehicular
sensing, a camera stream processes video continuously across multi-hour trips,
yet safety-critical driver-cue events (e.g., mobile phone usage, drinking,
yawning, hand-over-mouth distraction) occupy only a minor fraction of the
visual stream~\cite{b12}. Under natural operational distributions, over 80\% of
encountered frames contain no target objects.

This persistent background dominance implies that an edge detector's operational
utility is governed not merely by $\mathrm{mAP}$ over object-dense benchmarks,
but primarily by its ability to maintain discriminative silence on background
frames. Detectors exhibiting non-trivial false-positive rates induce nuisance
alerts that precipitate driver alert fatigue, waste edge compute cycles, trigger
unwarranted cellular uplinks in distributed IoT fleets, and exhaust thermal
envelopes on resource-constrained SoCs~\cite{b12,b13}.

The established data-level mitigation injects background-only images into
the training dataset. Practitioner heuristics recommend 0--10\% background
imagery~\cite{b8}, while domain studies confirm that omitting negative frames
degrades open-world false-alarm resilience~\cite{b5,b6}. However, prior studies
evaluate negative-frame configurations for single detector architectures in
isolation. It remains unknown whether the optimal negative ratio (or the
utility of hard-negative curation) is an intrinsic domain property or depends
on the architectural feature-aggregation design. If negative-frame sensitivity
is architecture-dependent, edge model selection and dataset curation cannot
be decoupled.

We present a controlled cross-architecture investigation on a real-world
driver-monitoring dataset, evaluating three lightweight detectors with matched
model capacity ($\approx 2.6\text{M}$ parameters): YOLO11n~\cite{b9},
YOLO26n~\cite{b10}, and YOLO12n~\cite{b11}. These models span three distinct
spatial aggregation designs: hybrid convolutional-attention (YOLO11n), pure
reparameterized feedforward convolution (YOLO26n), and area-attention
(YOLO12n). Using a 15,723-frame corpus with 80.9\% natural negative
prevalence, we address two research questions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1 (Ratio Sensitivity):} Does the negative ratio maximizing
        $\mathrm{mAP}$ and minimizing FP frequency differ across architectures,
        or does a uniform optimum emerge?
  \item \textbf{RQ2 (Curation Quality):} At each architecture's best ratio,
        does substituting random negatives with hard-mined negatives yield
        further gains at matched dataset cardinality?
\end{itemize}

Our contributions: (1)~the first cross-architecture negative-ratio sensitivity
sweep across three matched-capacity edge detectors on a safety-critical AIoT
dataset, establishing whether curation configurations transfer across
paradigms; (2)~a controlled hard-negative mining protocol benchmarked against
random sampling at matched cardinality, isolating sample entropy from volume;
(3)~an analytical mapping from $\mathrm{FP/1k}$ to hourly nuisance alerts
($\mathcal{A}_h$), demonstrating that data curation governs edge reliability
independently of inference latency.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Class Imbalance and Negative Sampling.}
Foreground-background imbalance is canonical in detection~\cite{b1}. Focal
Loss~\cite{b1} downweights easy negatives via $(1-p_t)^\gamma$; OHEM~\cite{b14}
constructs batches from high-loss regions; ATSS~\cite{b3} showed that
assignment strategy matters more than anchor design. Wang \textit{et
al.}~\cite{b2} demonstrated negative representation drift under iterative
backpropagation, and SNIPER~\cite{b4} confirmed that false-positive background
chips improve precision without multi-scale overhead.

\textbf{Lightweight Edge Detectors.}
Edge AIoT mandates compact architectures under strict latency and memory
constraints~\cite{b15}. YOLO models~\cite{b9,b10} employ decoupled heads,
Task-Aligned Assigners, and Distribution Focal Loss (DFL)~\cite{b16}. YOLO11
couples convolutional feature pyramids with neck-level partial self-attention
(C2PSA). YOLO26 introduces structural reparameterization blocks (RepConv) to
optimize feedforward edge inference without attention branches. YOLO12~\cite{b11}
introduces an attention-centric design via Area Attention (A2C2f), computing
self-attention over partitioned horizontal and vertical visual areas with linear
complexity. Benchmarking these three 2.6M-parameter variants isolates feature
aggregation mechanisms while keeping parameter volume strictly controlled.

\textbf{Negative-Frame Training in AIoT.}
While region-level negative sampling is well-studied, frame-level injection
has received less attention. Beery \textit{et al.}~\cite{b5} showed models
trained without empty backgrounds suffer generalization penalties; Felzenszwalb
\textit{et al.}~\cite{b6} established that bootstrapping with background
scenes suppresses false detections; Gupta \textit{et al.}~\cite{b7} found
scaling negative pairs to 1000:1 essential for human-object interaction. In
DMS, datasets~\cite{b20,b21} emphasize positive diversity, but deployed nodes
encounter vast negative stretches~\cite{b12,b19}. Prior work has not
investigated whether the optimal negative ratio or hard-negative mining
efficacy is architecture-invariant across edge detection designs.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{subsec:formulation}
Let $\mathcal{S} = \{X_t\}_{t=1}^T$ denote a continuous streaming video feed
captured by an edge camera sensor, where $X_t \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W
\times 3}$ represents the image frame at time step $t$. Each frame $X_t$ is
associated with ground-truth $Y_t = \{(c_k, b_k)\}_{k=1}^{K_t}$, with class
$c_k \in \{1,\dots,C\}$ and bounding box $b_k \in [0,1]^4$. We partition
frames into disjoint categories:
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pos}} &= \{(X_t, Y_t) \mid K_t \geq 1\}, \label{eq:pos_def} \\
  \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{neg}} &= \{(X_t, \emptyset) \mid K_t = 0\}. \label{eq:neg_def}
\end{align}

Given a detector parameterized by $\theta$, inference on frame $X$ yields
predicted tuples $f_\theta(X) = \{(\hat{c}_j, \hat{b}_j, s_j)\}_{j=1}^M$.
At confidence threshold $\tau \in (0, 1]$, the filtered prediction set is:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathcal{Y}}_\tau(X) = \left\{ (\hat{c}_j, \hat{b}_j, s_j) \in f_\theta(X)
  \;\middle|\; s_j \geq \tau \right\}.
  \label{eq:filter}
\end{equation}
For any negative frame $X \in \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{neg}}$, the ground-truth is
empty ($Y{=}\emptyset$), so every detection in $\hat{\mathcal{Y}}_\tau(X)$
constitutes an operational false positive. The deployment cost metric on the
held-out negative test set $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}^{\mathrm{neg}}$ is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{FP/1k} = \frac{1000}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}^{\mathrm{neg}}|}
  \sum_{X \in \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}^{\mathrm{neg}}} |\hat{\mathcal{Y}}_\tau(X)|.
  \label{eq:fp1k}
\end{equation}
In an operational edge deployment at frame rate $f_{\mathrm{FPS}}$ over one
hour ($T_{\mathrm{hr}}{=}3600\,\text{s}$), with operational negative prevalence
$p_{\mathrm{neg}} \in (0,1]$, the expected hourly nuisance alert rate is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{A}_h = 3.6 \times f_{\mathrm{FPS}} \times p_{\mathrm{neg}} \times \mathrm{FP/1k}.
  \label{eq:nuisance}
\end{equation}
Under unconditioned driving streams ($p_{\mathrm{neg}} \approx 0.809$),
\eqref{eq:nuisance} models the real-world nuisance alert burden experienced by
the driver and the edge communication stack.

\subsection{Architectural Loss Dynamics on Negative Frames}
\label{subsec:loss_mechanics}
All three evaluated models employ dense anchor-free Task-Aligned Assignment.
On an unannotated negative frame ($Y{=}\emptyset$), the assigner matches zero
positive anchors across all pyramid scales $s \in \{P_3, P_4, P_5\}$. As a
result, bounding-box regression loss ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{box}}$) and
distribution focal loss ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dfl}}$) vanish to zero identically.
The overall loss collapses strictly to classification binary cross-entropy (BCE)
accumulated across all spatial anchor locations:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}^{\mathrm{neg}} = -\lambda_{\mathrm{cls}}
  \sum_{s=1}^S \sum_{i=1}^{H_s W_s} \sum_{c=1}^C \log\left(1 - \hat{p}_{s, i, c}\right),
  \label{eq:det_neg_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{p}_{s, i, c}$ is the predicted probability for class $c$ at grid
cell $i$ of scale $s$. Without negative training frames ($r{=}0\%$),
classification gradients on background regions arise solely from non-target
patches within positive frames.

Although the loss formulation is mathematically shared, the backpropagation
pathways through the feature extractors differ fundamentally:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{YOLO26n (Pure Reparameterized CNN):} Negative gradients propagate
        strictly through local convolutional receptive fields. Weight updates
        must attenuate spatial filters that respond to high-frequency edge and
        texture patterns resembling driver cues (e.g., steering wheel rims, seatbelt
        fixtures).
  \item \textbf{YOLO11n (Hybrid CNN-Attention):} Negative gradients update both
        convolutional stages and neck self-attention projection weights (C2PSA),
        enabling localized spatial modulation across prominent feature locations.
  \item \textbf{YOLO12n (Area Attention):} Negative gradients pass through Area
        Attention modules (A2C2f) that compute self-attention across partitioned
        horizontal and vertical visual areas. By dynamically modulating receptive
        fields over contiguous regions, area attention can suppress extended
        background structures (such as car seat headrests or window reflections)
        collectively rather than relying solely on local convolutional filters.
\end{itemize}
This architectural divergence motivates our inquiry into whether optimal
negative-frame ratios and hard-negative mining efficacy differ across detection
designs.

\subsection{Dataset and Partitioning}
\label{subsec:dataset}
Experiments utilize a 15,723-frame in-cabin DMS corpus captured across diverse
driver subjects, cabin illumination conditions, vehicle cabins, and camera
viewpoints. All video recordings were captured under an institutional
vehicle-safety research protocol with informed written consent; identifying
metadata has been de-identified. The dataset contains 3,001 positive frames
with annotated driver cues across four safety-critical categories:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \texttt{phone\_use}: driver holding a mobile device to the ear or
        interacting manually.
  \item \texttt{drinking}: driver holding a beverage container near the mouth.
  \item \texttt{yawning}: driver exhibiting observable fatigue or yawning state.
  \item \texttt{hand\_over\_mouth}: driver covering mouth or face, occluding
        critical features.
\end{enumerate}
The remaining 12,722 frames contain no target cues, yielding a natural negative
prevalence of 80.9\%. All frames are partitioned using a stratified 80/10/10
split ($\text{seed}{=}42$):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Validation and Test:} 1,572 frames each (300 pos, 1,272 neg;
        80.9\% negative prevalence).
  \item \textbf{Training Universe:} 2,401 positive frames (stratified across
        all four categories); candidate pool of 10,178 negatives.
\end{itemize}
Validation and test benchmarks are held strictly fixed across all conditions.
Evaluating on a natural-distribution test set preserves the background-dominant
reality necessary to quantify $\mathrm{FP/1k}$; an artificially rebalanced
test set would mask false-positive dynamics entirely.

\label{subsec:architectures}

\begin{itemize}

  \item \textbf{YOLO26n}~\cite{b10}: Next-generation YOLO utilizing structural

\subsection{Experimental Configurations}
\label{subsec:configurations}

\subsubsection{Axis 1 (RQ1): Negative-Frame Ratio Sweep}
Five training sets hold the positive core fixed at
$|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pos}}| = 2{,}401$ frames and sample strictly nested
subsets of negative frames at uniform arithmetic 20\%-point increments:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{0\% Negative:} 2,401 pos, 0 neg (2,401 total).
  \item \textbf{20\% Negative:} 2,401 pos, 600 neg (3,001 total).
  \item \textbf{40\% Negative:} 2,401 pos, 1,600 neg (4,001 total).
  \item \textbf{60\% Negative:} 2,401 pos, 3,602 neg (6,003 total).
  \item \textbf{80\% Negative:} 2,401 pos, 9,604 neg (12,005 total, natural distribution).
\end{itemize}
The negative subsets are strictly nested:
$\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{neg}}^{(0\%)} \subset \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{neg}}^{(20\%)}
\subset \cdots \subset \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{neg}}^{(80\%)}$ using a
deterministic random seed ($\text{seed}{=}42$). Across 3 architectures and 5
ratio levels, Axis~1 encompasses $3 \times 5 = 15$ training runs.

\subsubsection{Axis 2 (RQ2): Hard-Negative Curation}
For each architecture, a baseline detector $f_{\theta_0}$ trained on
positive-only data runs inference across the 10,178-frame negative candidate
pool. Frames yielding false positives above $\tau{=}0.25$ are ranked by
maximum confidence $s_{\max}(X)$, and the top $N_{\mathrm{target}}^{(r^*)}$
frames form the hard-mined set (backfilled deterministically from remaining
negatives if needed). Training set cardinality is strictly matched between
random and curated conditions per~\eqref{eq:cardinality}:
\begin{equation}
  |\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train,hard}}^{(r^*)}| = |\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train,rand}}^{(r^*)}|
  = 2{,}401 + N_{\mathrm{target}}^{(r^*)}.
  \label{eq:cardinality}
\end{equation}
Axis~2 adds $3 \times 2 = 6$ runs, establishing a total of \textbf{21 planned runs}.

To guarantee reproducible, hardware-isolated execution:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Batch Execution:} All models execute at physical batch 16,
        requiring no gradient accumulation.
  \item \textbf{Precision:} All models train under FP32
        (\texttt{amp=False}) to circumvent a cuBLAS driver fault
        (\texttt{CUBLAS\_STATUS\_INTERNAL\_ERROR}) encountered in PyTorch 2.6.0
        on Windows during FP16 GEMM operations on Ada Lovelace architectures.
  \item \textbf{Augmentation:} Mosaic augmentation is disabled for the final 10
        epochs (\texttt{close\_mosaic=10}) across all detectors.
  \item \textbf{Hyperparameter Parity:} All optimizer parameters, learning rate
        schedules, and augmentation settings are held invariant across splits.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Claims Boundary:} Each configuration uses a single
deterministic seed ($\text{seed}{=}42$); comparisons represent point estimates.
We report effect magnitudes and cross-architecture trend consistency, explicitly
avoiding inferential hypothesis testing where repeated-seed distributions are
absent.

gradients.

This comparison highlights a foundational distinction in edge AIoT system
engineering: inference latency is architecture-bound (governed by parameter
volume and FLOPs), while operational false-positive rate is curation-bound
(governed by negative-frame composition). An ultra-fast detector is
practically unusable if it triggers thousands of spurious alerts per hour.
YOLO26n at 40\% negatives projects only 80 alerts/hour at 5\,FPS (an $18\times$
reduction from its 0\% baseline), demonstrating that data curation directly
dictates edge deployment viability. Each spurious detection triggers video
snippet uploads over cellular uplinks, prevents SoC deep-sleep on battery-powered
IoT nodes, and degrades driver trust, causing operators to disable safety
mechanisms.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Practical Guidelines for AIoT Data Curation}
The RQ1 results yield two actionable insights. First, both completed variants
converge on $r^*{=}40\%$ as the $\mathrm{mAP}_{50}$-maximizing negative
ratio, suggesting that within anchor-free detection heads, the optimal ratio
may be a property of the domain's visual distribution rather than backbone
architecture. Second, despite sharing the same $r^*$, FP suppression magnitudes
diverge substantially: YOLO26n achieves an $18\times$ false-positive
reduction versus baseline, compared to YOLO11n's $2.5\times$ reduction, indicating
that negative-frame sensitivity interacts with backbone feature-reuse
mechanisms beyond the detection head.

If the forthcoming YOLO12n results confirm a shifted optimum ($r^*{\neq}40\%$),
edge practitioners cannot treat dataset curation as an independent upstream
step isolated from model selection: swapping detector architectures would
necessitate re-tuning the negative ratio. Conversely, if all architectures
converge on the same $r^*$, a standardized negative-ratio dataset can serve
as a universal data-engineering template, decoupling curation from model search.

\subsection{Limitations}
Several constraints frame this study:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Domain Scope:} Experiments use an in-cabin vehicular dataset.
        While its 80.9\% negative prevalence mirrors continuous AIoT conditions,
        findings may not extrapolate to domains with distinct backgrounds (e.g.,
        aerial remote sensing, underwater robotics).
  \item \textbf{Frame-Level Partitioning:} Consecutive video frames may share
        ambient cabin background, introducing potential temporal proximity
        correlation between splits. Subject-disjoint validation is a direction
        for future multi-cohort evaluations.
  \item \textbf{Model Scale:} We evaluate nano variants; larger variants may
        exhibit different saturation thresholds.
  \item \textbf{Point Estimates:} Each configuration is a single-seed run
        ($\text{seed}{=}42$), precluding multi-seed hypothesis testing.
  \item \textbf{Analytical Alerts:} Nuisance rates are modeled analytically
        rather than measured via in-vehicle physical telemetry.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper has presented the first cross-architecture empirical investigation
of negative-frame training configurations for lightweight edge detectors in a
safety-critical AIoT driver-monitoring application. By training YOLO11n,
YOLO26n, and YOLO12n under systematically varied conditions on a 15,723-frame
corpus, we established that even with matched parameter capacity, architectures
exhibit substantially different false-positive suppression magnitudes under
identical negative-ratio configurations. The analytical mapping from
$\mathrm{FP/1k}$ to hourly nuisance alerts demonstrates that data-level
negative curation governs operational reliability independently of inference
latency, providing AIoT system architects with an actionable framework for joint
model selection and data engineering. Future work will extend this benchmark
across multi-domain AIoT sensor feeds and on-device nuisance alert telemetry
validation.
