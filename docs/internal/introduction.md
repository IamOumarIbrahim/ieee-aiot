I. Introduction

Object detectors deployed in AIoT systems (such as in-cabin driver monitoring, smart surveillance, and industrial defect inspection) operate under a condition that standard computer vision benchmarks rarely reflect: the vast majority of frames a model encounters at inference time contain no target object at all. A driver-monitoring camera streams continuously for hours per trip, yet the events it must flag (distraction, drowsiness, unsafe behavior) occupy a small fraction of that stream. This asymmetry means a detector's behavior on background-only frames, rather than merely its accuracy on object-dense benchmark images, determines whether the system is usable in the field. A model prone to false positives on ordinary, uneventful footage produces nuisance alerts that erode user trust, waste edge compute cycles, and, in bandwidth- or battery-constrained AIoT deployments, trigger unnecessary uplink transmissions or actuator responses.

The standard mitigation is to include negative (background-only) frames during training, and prior work confirms this lever is powerful: reported optimal negative-frame proportions range from single digits [Ultralytics guidance] to over 60% of a training set in a safety-critical driving context [roadwork ref], with performance following a non-monotonic, inverted-U relationship: too few negatives leave false positives unaddressed, while too many suppress recall by pushing the detector toward over-conservative predictions [debris ref]. However, every empirical study we are aware of tunes this parameter for a single detector architecture in isolation. It remains unknown whether a negative-frame configuration (including ratio or the choice between random and hard-mined negatives) tuned for one architecture transfers to another, or whether the optimum is itself architecture-dependent. This is not a minor gap: AIoT practitioners routinely choose among CNN-based, anchor-free, and transformer-based lightweight detectors under a shared, fixed data-curation budget, and if the answer to "how many negative frames do I need" changes with architecture choice, then architecture and data-curation decisions cannot be made independently, as much current practice implicitly assumes.

This paper addresses that gap through a controlled, cross-architecture study of negative-frame configuration on a real-world, safety-critical AIoT dataset. Using an in-cabin driver-monitoring corpus with a naturally occurring 19:81 positive-to-negative frame ratio, we train three lightweight detectors spanning distinct design paradigms (YOLO11n, YOLO26n, and the transformer-based D-FINE-N) under systematically varied negative-frame ratios and sampling strategies, holding all other training factors fixed. We ask:

RQ1: Does the negative-frame ratio that maximizes detection performance differ across detector architectures, or does a single configuration generalize?

RQ2: At each architecture's best ratio, does replacing randomly sampled negatives with hard-mined negatives (frames on which a baseline model already produces false positives) yield further, architecture-dependent gains?

Our contributions are threefold:

A cross-architecture benchmark of negative-frame ratio sensitivity on a real AIoT dataset, testing whether the inverted-U relationship reported for individual architectures in prior work holds consistently across CNN-based and transformer-based lightweight detectors, or whether the optimal ratio and the detector's sensitivity to deviating from it are themselves architecture-dependent.
A hard-negative curation protocol and cost-benefit comparison against random negative sampling at matched dataset size, quantifying whether the additional curation effort is justified, and whether that answer differs by architecture.
A deployment-cost reframing of false-positive rate, translating measured FP-per-frame differences into an estimated nuisance-alert rate per hour of continuous operation, connecting an accuracy-only metric to a consequence AIoT system designers actually plan around, while explicitly noting that negative-frame configuration affects this rate but not inference latency, which remains fixed by architecture alone.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews related work on negative sampling in object detection. Section III details our dataset, architectures, and experimental design. Section IV presents experimental results addressing RQ1, RQ2, and the operational deployment-cost translation. Section V discusses practical implications and limitations for AIoT practitioners, and Section VI concludes.
