# Section I: Introduction

Object detectors deployed in edge Artificial Intelligence of Things (AIoT) ecosystems---including in-cabin driver monitoring systems (DMS), autonomous robotic platforms, and persistent industrial surveillance nodes---operate under an operational regime that conventional computer vision benchmarks rarely reflect: extreme temporal class imbalance [1]. In an in-cabin vehicular sensing node, a camera stream processes video continuously across multi-hour trips, yet safety-critical driver-cue events (e.g., mobile phone usage, drinking, fatigue yawning, hand-over-mouth distraction) occupy only a minor fraction of the visual stream [12]. Under natural operational distributions, over 80% of encountered frames contain no target objects whatsoever.

This persistent background dominance implies that an edge detector's operational utility is governed not merely by its mean Average Precision ($\mathrm{mAP}$) over object-dense benchmark imagery, but primarily by its ability to maintain discriminative silence on background-only (negative) frames. Detectors that exhibit non-trivial false-positive ($\mathrm{FP}$) rates on background frames induce frequent nuisance alerts [6]. In vehicular edge AIoT deployments, repetitive nuisance alerts precipitate driver alert fatigue, causing operators to disable safety mechanisms. Concurrently, each spurious detection consumes edge processing cycles, triggers unwarranted cellular uplink transmissions in distributed IoT fleets, and exhausts battery or thermal envelopes on resource-constrained embedded system-on-chips (SoCs) [13].

The established data-level mitigation is to inject background-only images into the detector's training dataset. Prior single-architecture investigations confirm that this is a potent regularization lever: reported optimal negative proportions range from practitioner heuristics of 0–10% [8] to upwards of 60% in safety-critical vehicular environments [6]. Empirical sweeps demonstrate that detector response to negative-frame prevalence follows a non-monotonic, inverted-U trajectory [5]: insufficient negative representation leaves background false positives unpenalized, whereas an excessive negative ratio dilutes gradient updates on rare foreground classes, driving the detector toward over-conservative predictions and suppressing recall.

However, all existing studies evaluate negative-frame configurations for a single detector architecture in isolation. It remains fundamentally unknown whether the optimal negative-frame ratio---or the utility of active hard-negative curation over uniform random sampling---is an intrinsic property of the dataset domain or whether it depends on the underlying architectural paradigm (e.g., anchor-free convolutional feature pyramids versus query-based transformer attention). In modern AIoT practice, system architects frequently choose among diverse lightweight detector families under a fixed data-engineering and curation budget. If optimal negative-frame sensitivity is architecture-dependent, model selection and training-data curation cannot be decoupled, as current deployment workflows implicitly assume.

To resolve this question, this paper presents a controlled, cross-architecture investigation of negative-frame configuration on a real-world, safety-critical AIoT driver-monitoring dataset. We evaluate three lightweight edge detectors spanning distinct architectural lineages: YOLO11n (anchor-free CNN) [9], YOLO26n (next-generation CNN with reparameterized convolution blocks) [10], and D-FINE-N (real-time DETR transformer with fine-grained distribution refinement) [11]. Using a 15,723-frame driver-monitoring corpus with a natural prevalence of 80.9% negative frames, we systematically vary the negative training ratio across five nested levels (0% to 80%) and evaluate false-positive hard-negative mining against random negative sampling at matched dataset cardinality. We address two formal research questions:
* **RQ1 (Ratio Sensitivity and Paradigm Invariance):** Does the negative-frame ratio that maximizes detection accuracy ($\mathrm{mAP}$) and minimizes false-positive frequency differ across detector architectures, or does a uniform, architecture-invariant optimum emerge?
* **RQ2 (Curation Quality vs. Sample Volume):** At each architecture's empirically identified best ratio, does substituting random negative frames with hard-mined negative frames (background scenes inducing false positives in a baseline model) yield further performance gains at matched dataset cardinality, and does this benefit vary between CNN and transformer architectures?

Our core contributions are threefold:
1. **Cross-Architecture Negative-Ratio Benchmark:** We deliver the first controlled empirical sensitivity sweep of negative-frame training ratios across three lightweight edge detectors spanning CNN and transformer paradigms on a safety-critical AIoT in-cabin monitoring dataset, establishing whether curation configurations transfer across detector lineages.
2. **Controlled Hard-Negative Curation Protocol:** We design an empirical false-positive mining protocol benchmarked against uniform random sampling at strictly matched dataset size, isolating the contribution of sample information entropy from sample volume across diverse detector architectures.
3. **Operational Deployment Cost Reframing:** We formulate an analytical mapping translating measured false positives per 1,000 frames ($\mathrm{FP/1k}$) into estimated hourly nuisance alerts ($\mathcal{A}_h$), demonstrating that data-level negative curation governs edge operational reliability independently of architectural inference latency.
